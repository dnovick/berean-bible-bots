# BBH Chapter 22 — Qal Participle

*Source: Pratico & Van Pelt, Basics of Biblical Hebrew Grammar (BBH), Chapter 22*

---

## 22.1 Introduction (§22.1)

In English, Participles are formed by adding "-ing" to a verb as in "walking," "eating," or "sleeping." Because a Participle is a verbal adjective, it shares the characteristics of both verbs and adjectives. As a verb, the Participle has stem (Qal) and voice (active or passive) and expresses some type of verbal action, such as "running" or "studying." Like a verb, it can also take a direct object. As an adjective, the Participle has gender (masculine or feminine) and number (singular or plural) and is used much like an adjective: attributively (the consuming fire), predicatively (the fire is consuming), or substantively (the one who consumes). When translating the Hebrew Participle, begin by using the "-ing" form of the verb as in the above examples.

---

## 22.2 The Qal Active Participle Paradigm: Strong Verbs (§22.2)

The Participle inflects like an adjective, with both gender and number. It is not inflected for person. Note that there are two feminine singular forms. The following paradigm must be memorized:

| | Singular | Plural |
|---|---|---|
| **Masculine** | קֹטֵל | קֹטְלִים |
| **Feminine** | קֹטֶ֫לֶת | קֹטְלוֹת |
| **Feminine (alt)** | קֹטְלָה | |

---

## 22.3 Notes on the Qal Active Participle Paradigm: Strong Verbs (§22.3)

1. Participles are not inflected for person, only for gender and number. The inflectional endings for the Participle are the same as those memorized for adjectives and nouns, except for the feminine singular form קֹטֶ֫לֶת.
2. There are two feminine singular forms. The feminine singular קֹטֶ֫לֶת is more irregular than קֹטְלָה but occurs more frequently. Both forms must be memorized.
3. The diagnostic feature of the Qal active Participle is the Holem following the first root letter in all Participle forms (קֹטֵל). This vowel may also be written as Holem Waw (קוֹטֵל). The Holem-Tsere vowel pattern is distinctive of the masculine singular Qal active Participle:

| Qal Active Participle |
|---|
| קֹטֵל |

## 22.4 The Qal Active Participle: Weak Verbs (§22.4)

In the Qal stem, I-Guttural, I-נ, I-י, and Geminate weak verbs follow the strong verb pattern:

|---|---|---|

| **Feminine** | עֹמֶ֫דֶת | עֹמְדוֹת |
| **Feminine (alt)** | עֹמְדָה | |

| | Singular | Plural |
|---|---|---|

| **Feminine** | נֹפֶ֫לֶת | נֹפְלוֹת |
| **Feminine (alt)** | נֹפְלָה | |

**I-י (יָשַׁב):**

| | Singular | Plural |
|---|---|---|
| **Masculine** | יֹשֵׁב | יֹשְׁבִים |
| **Feminine** | יֹשֶׁ֫בֶת | יֹשְׁבוֹת |
| **Feminine (alt)** | יֹשְׁבָה | |

**Geminate (סָבַב):**

| | Singular | Plural |
|---|---|---|
| **Masculine** | סֹבֵב | סֹבְבִים |
| **Feminine** | סֹבֶ֫בֶת | סֹבְבוֹת |
| **Feminine (alt)** | סֹבְבָה | |

Other weak verb roots, however, deviate from this strong verb pattern. In every weak verb class except Biconsonantal, the diagnostic Holem vowel and inflectional endings are unchanged.

### 1 — II-Guttural Verbs

The presence of a guttural consonant in second root position creates only minor spelling differences. In one of the feminine singular and both plural forms, Vocal Shewa is expected under the second root consonant (קֹטְלָה). With II-Guttural verbal roots, the guttural takes Hateph Pathach (בֹּחֲרָה or שֹׁאֲלִים). The diagnostic Holem vowel is preserved in every form.

**II-Guttural (בָּחַר):**

| | Singular | Plural |
|---|---|---|
| **Masculine** | בֹּחֵר | בֹּחֲרִים |
| **Feminine** | בֹּחֶ֫רֶת | בֹּחֲרוֹת |
| **Feminine (alt)** | בֹּחֲרָה | |

**II-Guttural I-א (שָׁאַל):**

| | Singular | Plural |
|---|---|---|
| **Masculine** | שֹׁאֵל | שֹׁאֲלִים |
| **Feminine** | שֹׁאֶ֫לֶת | שֹׁאֲלוֹת |
| **Feminine (alt)** | שֹׁאֲלָה | |

### 2 — III-ע/ח Verbs

In this weak verb class, only the feminine singular form varies from the strong verb pattern: שֹׁלַ֫חַת or שֹׁמַ֫עַת (vs. קֹטֶ֫לֶת). This change is due to gutturals preferring a-class vowels. Note the Furtive Pathach in the masculine singular forms (שֹׁלֵחַ and שֹׁמֵעַ).

**III-ח (שָׁלַח):**

| | Singular | Plural |
|---|---|---|
| **Masculine** | שֹׁלֵחַ | שֹׁלְחִים |
| **Feminine** | שֹׁלַ֫חַת | שֹׁלְחוֹת |
| **Feminine (alt)** | שֹׁלְחָה | |

**III-ע (שָׁמַע):**

| | Singular | Plural |
|---|---|---|
| **Masculine** | שֹׁמֵעַ | שֹׁמְעִים |
| **Feminine** | שֹׁמַ֫עַת | שֹׁמְעוֹת |
| **Feminine (alt)** | שֹׁמְעָה | |

### 3 — III-א Verbs

Only the feminine singular מֹצֵ֫את varies from the strong verb pattern, where the א is quiescent. The remaining III-א forms follow the strong verb pattern.

**III-א (מָצָא):**

| | Singular | Plural |
|---|---|---|
| **Masculine** | מֹצֵא | מֹצְאִים |
| **Feminine** | מֹצֵ֫את | מֹצְאוֹת |
| **Feminine (alt)** | מֹצְאָה | |

### 4 — III-ה Verbs

In this weak class, the final root consonant (ה) is lost in every form. In the singular forms, the presence of the vowel letter ה helps with identification. Be careful to distinguish between the masculine singular בֹּנֶה and the feminine singular בֹּנָה. The feminine singular בֹּנִיָּה is not well-attested (only ten times) and is included to complete the paradigm but not for memorization. In the plural forms, no ה vowel letter appears to help with identification — only mastery of the paradigm will enable recognition.

**III-ה (בָּנָה):**

| | Singular | Plural |
|---|---|---|
| **Masculine** | בֹּנֶה | בֹּנִים |
| **Feminine** | בֹּנָה | בֹּנוֹת |
| **Feminine (alt)** | בֹּנִיָּה | |

### 5 — Biconsonantal Verbs

Only this class of weak verbs appears without the diagnostic Holem vowel of the Qal Participle. Memorization of the following paradigm is suggested. Note that the masculine singular Participle קָם (rising) is identical to the Qal Perfect 3ms form קָם (he arose). Similarly, the feminine singular Participle קָמָה (rising) is distinguished from the Qal Perfect 3fs form קָ֫מָה (she arose) only by the accent. Context (and the accent) will enable the correct choice.

**Biconsonantal (קוּם):**

| | Singular | Plural |
|---|---|---|
| **Masculine** | קָם | קָמִים |
| **Feminine** | קָמָה | קָמוֹת |

## 22.5 The Use of the Qal Active Participle (§22.5)

In Hebrew, Participles function just like adjectives in that either may be used attributively, predicatively, or substantively.

### 1 — Attributive Use

When a Participle directly modifies a noun, it is being used attributively. The Hebrew Participle follows the noun that it modifies and agrees with the noun in gender, number, and definiteness. The attributive Participle may also be translated with a relative clause ("the giant who is sleeping").

> בָּאָרֶץ הַיֹּשֵׁב הָעָם
> the people who dwell (who are dwelling) in the land (Num 13:28)

> אֹכְלָה יְהוָה אֱלֹהֶיךָ אֵשׁ
> Yahweh your God is a consuming fire (Deut 4:24).

### 2 — Predicative Use

This use of the Participle asserts something about a noun without directly modifying it. In Hebrew, the predicative relationship is indicated when a Participle agrees with the noun in gender and number but not definiteness. In this usage, the Participle never takes the definite article. The Participle usually follows the noun and is a "tenseless" verb form — its tense must be taken from context.

> בָּאֵשׁ בֹּעֵר וְהָהָר
> And the mountain was burning with fire (Deut 4:11).

> בְּהֵיכַל שֹׁכֵב וּשְׁמוּאֵל
> And Samuel was lying down in (the) temple (1 Sam 3:3).

### 3 — Substantive Use

A Participle may be used independently as a noun, indicating the "one who" performs a certain action. When used substantively, it behaves just like a noun: it may function as the subject or object of a verb, take a definite article, appear in a construct chain, or take pronominal suffixes and prepositional prefixes. Several Hebrew Participles are used so frequently as substantives that they are commonly listed as nouns: כֹּהֵן (priest), אֹיֵב (enemy), and שֹׁפֵט (judge) all retain the Holem-Tsere vowel pattern of the masculine singular Participle.

> בַּגַּנַּים הַיּוֹשֶׁבֶת
> the one who dwells in the gardens (Song 8:13)

> יִשְׂרָאֵל שׁוֹמֵר וְלֹא יִישָׁן
> and the one who keeps Israel will not sleep (Ps 121:4)

## 22.6 The Qal Passive Participle (§22.6)

In addition to the Qal active Participle, there is also a Qal passive Participle in Hebrew. With verbs having an active voice, the subject performs the action (David killed Goliath). With verbs having a passive voice, the subject receives the verbal action (Goliath was killed by David). When translating a passive Participle, the "-ing" ending appears on the helping verb ("being killed") or an English form like "blessed" may be used.

---

## 22.7 The Qal Passive Participle Paradigm: Strong Verbs (§22.7)

The passive Participle, like the active Participle, is inflected in four forms. Unlike the active Participle, however, there is only one feminine singular form. Participles (active and passive) are not inflected for person, only gender and number.

| | Singular | Plural |
|---|---|---|
| **Masculine** | קָטוּל | קְטוּלִים |
| **Feminine** | קְטוּלָה | קְטוּלוֹת |

---

## 22.8 Notes on the Qal Passive Participle Paradigm: Strong Verbs (§22.8)

1. The diagnostic feature of the Qal passive Participle is the Shureq stem vowel (קָטוּל). It may also be spelled defectively with Qibbuts (קָטֻל):

| Qal Passive Participle |
|---|
| קָטוּל |

2. In forms with inflectional endings (fs, mp, fp), a Vocal Shewa appears under the first root consonant due to propretonic reduction (קְטוּלָה). With I-Guttural verbal roots, this Vocal Shewa changes to Hateph Pathach: עֲזוּבָה (from עָזַב, to leave, forsake, abandon).

3. The passive Participle of most weak verbs can be recognized without difficulty — the strong verb vowel pattern is present in every form. The following are masculine singular examples only:

| Form | Gloss |
|---|---|
| עָזוּב | abandoned |
| פָּתוּחַ | opened |
| שָׂנוּא | hated |
| יָדוּעַ | known |
| שָׁאוּל | asked |
| נָטוּעַ | planted |
| שָׁחוּט | slaughtered |
| אָרוּר | cursed |
| אָכוּל | eaten |
| יָעוּץ | advised |

4. The form of the III-ה passive Participle is quite unusual. In every form, the consonant י stands in the place of the third root consonant, reminiscent of the fact that III-ה verbs were originally III-י. For example, נְטוּיָה is the feminine singular Qal passive Participle from נָטָה (to stretch out). Think of this י as diagnostic of the III-ה class.

**III-ה passive Participle (בָּנָה):**

| | Singular | Plural |
|---|---|---|
| **Masculine** | בָּנוּי | בְּנוּיִים |
| **Feminine** | בְּנוּיָה | בְּנוּיוֹת |

---

## 22.9 The Use of the Qal Passive Participle (§22.9)

Passive Participles, like active Participles, may be used attributively, predicatively, or substantively.

### 1 — Attributive Use

The passive Participle follows the noun it modifies and agrees in gender, number, and definiteness.

> בְּסֵפֶר הַתּוֹרָה הַזֶּה הַכְּתוּבָה הַבְּרִית
> the covenant written in this book of the law (Deut 29:20 [English 29:21])

> הַפְּרוּצָה וַיִּבֶן אֶת־כָּל־הַחוֹמָה
> (And) he (re)built all of the broken wall (2 Chr 32:5).

### 2 — Predicative Use

The passive Participle either precedes or follows the noun it modifies and agrees in gender and number only (not definiteness).

> יְהוָה לְעוֹלָם בָּרוּךְ
> Blessed is Yahweh forever (Ps 89:53 [English 89:52]).

> הָאֲדָמָה בַּעֲבוּרֶךָ אֲרוּרָה
> Cursed is the ground because of you (Gen 3:17).

### 3 — Substantive Use

Passive Participles may be used independently as nouns, indicating the "one who" performs a certain action. When used substantively, they behave just like nouns.

> הַיְלוּדִים וְאֵלֶּה שְׁמוֹת
> And these are the names of the ones who were born (1 Chr 14:4).

> בְּתוֹרַת מֹשֶׁה כַּכָּתוּב
> according to what is written in the law of Moses (2 Chr 23:18)

---

## 22.10 Parsing the Active and Passive Participle (§22.10)

Like a verb, the Participle has stem and voice. Like an adjective, it is inflected for gender and number. Participles do not have person. When parsing, identify stem, voice, conjugation, gender, number, and lexical form.

| Form | Parsing | Lexical Form |
|---|---|---|
| יֹשֵׁב | Qal Active Participle ms | יָשַׁב |
| בָּרוּךְ | Qal Passive Participle ms | בָּרַךְ |

---

## 22.11 Participles with Prefixes and Suffixes (§22.11)

Because Participles behave like nouns, they may take the definite article, prepositional prefixes, or pronominal suffixes. They may also be found in the construct state.

> בְרִיתוֹ לְשֹׁמְרֵי
> "to those who keep (who are keeping) his covenant" (Ps 103:18). The preposition לְ is prefixed to the Participle, which is in the construct state.

> בַּשָּׂדֶה הַהֹלֵךְ
> "the one who walks in the field" (Gen 24:65). Note the definite article on the Participle.

> רֹפְאֶךָ אֲנִי יְהוָה
> "I am Yahweh who heals you" (Exod 15:26). Note the 2ms object suffix (ךָ) on the Participle.

---

## 22.12 Summary (§22.12)

1. Participles are verbal adjectives. Like a verb, Participles have stem (Qal) and voice (active or passive) and may take a direct object. Like an adjective, they have gender and number. Participles do not have person.
2. The Qal active Participle paradigm for the strong verb must be memorized. The inflectional endings are the same as those memorized for adjectives and nouns (except for the irregular feminine singular form קֹטֶ֫לֶת):

| | Singular | Plural |
|---|---|---|
| **Masculine** | קֹטֵל | קֹטְלִים |
| **Feminine** | קֹטֶ֫לֶת | קֹטְלוֹת |
| **Feminine (alt)** | קֹטְלָה | |

3. A Holem following the first root letter is diagnostic of the Qal active Participle: קֹטֵל (also spelled קוֹטֵל).
4. The only class of Qal active Participles without the Holem vowel is the Biconsonantal weak verb class (§22.4.5). These weak verbs have a Qamets throughout (קָם).
5. The Qal passive Participle paradigm for the strong verb must be memorized:

| | Singular | Plural |
|---|---|---|
| **Masculine** | קָטוּל | קְטוּלִים |
| **Feminine** | קְטוּלָה | קְטוּלוֹת |

6. A Shureq stem vowel is diagnostic of the Qal passive Participle in every form: קָטוּל (also spelled defectively as קָטֻל).
7. Most weak verbs inflect like the strong verb in the active and passive Participle. However, III-ה weak verbs exhibit a number of irregularities. In both active and passive forms, the final root consonant (ה) has been lost:

| | Active Participle | | Passive Participle | |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| | **Singular** | **Plural** | **Singular** | **Plural** |
| **Masculine** | בֹּנֶה | בֹּנִים | בָּנוּי | בְּנוּיִים |
| **Feminine** | בֹּנָה | בֹּנוֹת | בְּנוּיָה | בְּנוּיוֹת |
| **Feminine (alt)** | בֹּנִיָּה | | | |

8. The three uses of the Participle (active and passive) are attributive, predicative, and substantive:
    a. **Attributive Use.** The Participle follows the noun that it modifies and agrees in gender, number, and definiteness.
    b. **Predicative Use.** The Participle either precedes or follows the noun that it modifies and agrees in gender and number only (not definiteness).
    c. **Substantive Use.** The Participle is used independently as a noun, indicating the "one who" performs a certain action. It may take the definite article, prepositional prefixes, or pronominal suffixes.

## 22.14 Additional Information: Adverbs (§22.14)

An adverb is a word used to modify, describe, limit, or qualify a verb, adjective, or another adverb. Hebrew adverbs are generally divided into four categories:

### 1 — Adverbs of Time

> יָדַעְתִּי כִּי־יְרֵא אֱלֹהִים אַתָּה עַתָּה
> Now I know that you fear God (Gen 22:12).

> יַקְהֵל שְׁלֹמֹה אֶת־זִקְנֵי יִשְׂרָאֵל אָז
> Then Solomon assembled the elders of Israel (1 Kgs 8:1).

> יַעֲבֹרוּ טֶרֶם וַיָּלִינוּ שָׁם
> They spent the night there before they crossed over (Josh 3:1).

### 2 — Adverbs of Place

> וַיֵּשֵׁבוּ שְׁבוּ־פֹה וַיֹּאמֶר
> And he said, "Sit here," and they sat (Ruth 4:2).

> וְשִׁמְעוּ הֵנָּה גֹּשׁוּ וַיֹּאמֶר יְהוֹשֻׁעַ אֶל־בְּנֵי יִשְׂרָאֵל
> And Joshua said to the people of Israel, "Approach here and listen" (Josh 3:9).

> אֶת־הָאָדָם אֲשֶׁר יָצָר שָׁם וַיָּשֶׂם
> "And he placed there the man whom he had formed" (Gen 2:8).

> מִחוּץ וְשַׂמְתָּ אֶת־הַשֻּׁלְחָן
> You will place the table outside (Exod 26:35).

### 3 — Adverbs of Degree

> מְאֹד וַיַּרְא אֱלֹהִים אֶת־כָּל־אֲשֶׁר עָשָׂה וְהִנֵּה־טוֹב
> And God saw everything that he had made and, behold, (it was) very good (Gen 1:31).

> וַתֵּלֶד בֵּן עוֹד וַתַּהַר
> And she conceived again and bore a son (Gen 29:33).

> אֶל־יְהוָה תָּמִיד עֵינַי
> My eyes are continually on Yahweh (Ps 25:15).

### 4 — Adverbs of Manner

> יַחְדָּו וַיַּעֲנוּ כָל־הָעָם
> And all the people answered together (Exod 19:8).

> פִּתְאֹם וַיָּבֹא אֲלֵיהֶם יְהוֹשֻׁעַ
> And Joshua came upon them suddenly (Josh 10:9).

## 22.15 Something You Should Know: A Still Small Voice? (§22.15)

> The prophet Elijah has a famous triumph over the prophets of Baal on Mt. Carmel (1 Kgs 18:17–40). The Lord shows who is truly God by answering the prophet's prayer with fire. For a time, God's people seem to repent. But in the next chapter, in a move of questionable courage, Elijah flees Queen Jezebel when she threatens to have him killed in retaliation. With angelic aid, he makes it to Mt. Horeb, where he encounters Yahweh in a storm theophany, much as Moses had long before. When Moses and Israel encountered Yahweh, he came in a dark cloud with "thunderous sounds" (קֹלֹת, Exod 19:16), such that the mountain trembled — as did the people! Now, however, Elijah meets God and hears, according to the traditional translations, "a still small voice" (קוֹל, KJV) or a "gentle whisper" (קוֹל, NIV). What is wrong with this picture?
>
> Whenever Yahweh appears in storm theophany in the Old Testament, the Hebrew word קוֹל is properly and logically translated "thunder," "thunderous voice," or the like. The translation in 1 Kgs 19:12 should be the same. The storm theophany genre leads us to expect it, and a study by J. Lust vindicates this expectation. Lust has shown that the key terms of the phrase, קוֹל דְּמָמָה דַקָּה (traditionally translated "still small voice" or the like) carry a very different meaning from that to which we are accustomed. The Hebrew קוֹל, of course, can mean "voice," "sound," "thunder," "thunderous voice," etc., depending on context. The theophanic context here would lead us to choose "thunderous voice." But what about the other terms? Lust has argued that דְּמָמָה comes from the root IIדמם (to roar). Likewise, דַקָּה comes from דקק (to crush, grind small). Traditionally, the adjective דַקָּה was interpreted figuratively, i.e., "made small, gentle." Lust, however, suggests the sense, "crushing." So, instead of a "still, small voice," Elijah hears a "roaring, crushing sound." Or, I would suggest, a "roaring, crushing, thunderous voice."
>
> Here is a case in which either translation is possible. But genre considerations make the new translation preferable. If it is to be adopted, the quality of Yahweh's voice makes perfect sense. His voice always sounds thunderous in storm theophany. But his voice is not merely thunder. God speaks words. And this one fact distinguishes him from Baal in a passage that is partly anti-Baal polemic. We read that Elijah encountered wind, earthquake, and fire, but that Yahweh was not in any of these. We would translate, "not yet." The Lord is not yet in any of these cosmic phenomena because they only precede and announce his coming, just as they did at Mt. Sinai (Exod 19:16–19). On the other hand, such phenomena are all that a Baal worshiper might ever expect of Baal, because Baal was himself the storm! What a difference: between worshiping the storm and the Maker of the storm — between worshiping the creature and the Creator!
