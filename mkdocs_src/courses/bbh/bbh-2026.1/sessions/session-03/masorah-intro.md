# Introduction to the Masorah

## What Is the Masorah?

The **Masorah** (מָסוֹרָה, also spelled *Massorah*) is the body of notes, annotations, and
traditions compiled by Jewish scribal scholars to preserve and transmit the exact text of the
Hebrew Bible. The word is related to the root מָסַר, "to hand down" or "to transmit" — the
Masorah is literally the *tradition handed down* about the sacred text.

The scholars who produced this work are called the **Masoretes** (מַסּוֹרָנִים). They were
active primarily between the 6th and 10th centuries CE, working in academies in
**Tiberias** (along the Sea of Galilee), **Babylon**, and **Palestine**.

---

## Why the Masorah Matters for You Right Now

Here is a striking fact: **the vowels you are learning this week do not appear in the
oldest biblical manuscripts**. The original consonantal text of the Hebrew Bible — what
scribes copied for centuries — contains only consonants. The vowel system you see in your
textbook and in printed Hebrew Bibles was invented and systematized by the Masoretes.

The cantillation marks (the accent marks that determine how the text is chanted in the
synagogue) were also added by the Masoretes — these are the marks that form the basis of
the cantillation diagrams we use in this course.

In short: every time you read a voweled Hebrew text, you are reading a Masoretic text.

## A Brief History

| Period | Development |
|---|---|
| ~200 BCE – 100 CE | The consonantal text is stabilized; multiple textual traditions exist |
| ~500–600 CE | Early Masoretic activity begins; competing systems of notation |
| ~600–900 CE | Three main schools emerge: Babylonian, Palestinian, and Tiberian |
| ~850–950 CE | **The Tiberian school** — especially the **Ben Asher family** — produces the standard text |
| ~920 CE | **Aleppo Codex** (*Keter Aram Tzova*) — the oldest nearly-complete Hebrew Bible, vocalized by Aaron ben Moses ben Asher |
| **1008/9 CE** | **Leningrad Codex** (*Codex Leningradensis*) — the oldest complete Hebrew Bible manuscript; the base text for *Biblia Hebraica Stuttgartensia* (BHS) and *Biblia Hebraica Quinta* (BHQ) |

The **Tiberian Masoretes** ultimately prevailed as the standard. Their vowel system —
which is what you are learning — is called the **Tiberian vocalization**.

---

## What the Masorah Contains

The Masoretic apparatus that accompanies printed Hebrew Bibles (such as BHS) has three main
components:

**Masorah Parva (Mp)** — Small notes printed in the outer margins of each page. They
record unusual features of a word: how many times it appears in the Bible, whether this is
the only occurrence (*hapax legomenon*), or whether this word has a special reading.

**Masorah Magna (Mm)** — More detailed notes, typically printed at the top and bottom
margins or in separate volumes. The Mm elaborates on the Mp's abbreviated notations with
full lists of references.

**Masorah Finalis** — Summaries and statistics gathered at the end of each biblical book
(e.g., the total number of verses, words, and letters; the middle verse of the book).

---

## Qere and Ketiv

One of the most practically important Masoretic conventions is the **Qere/Ketiv**
system. When the Masoretes believed the consonantal text was in error or required a
different pronunciation than what was written, they did not change the text itself —
instead:

- **Ketiv** (כְּתִיב, "what is written") — the original consonants remain in the text
- **Qere** (קְרֵי, "what is read") — the correct reading is noted in the margin, with
  its vowels placed beneath the Ketiv consonants

The most common example is the divine name **יהוה**: the consonants of the Tetragrammaton
appear in the text (Ketiv), but the vowels of *Adonai* (אֲדֹנָי) are placed beneath them,
signaling the reader to say *Adonai* rather than the divine name itself.

---

## The Standard Hebrew Text Today

When you use **Logos** or **Accordance** (or any major Bible software), the Hebrew text
you read is the **Masoretic Text** as preserved in the Leningrad Codex, edited in
*Biblia Hebraica Stuttgartensia* (BHS) or *Biblia Hebraica Quinta* (BHQ). The sigla
**MT** (Masoretic Text) refers to this tradition throughout scholarly literature.
