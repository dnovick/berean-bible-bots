# Chapter 2 — Hebrew Vowels

## 2.1 Hebrew Vowels

You might be surprised to learn that originally the Hebrew language had no written system of vowels. This does not mean, however, that Hebrew vowels did not exist. While they did not exist in written form, they had always been present in the spoken form of the language. If we were to take the vowels out of the English writing system, these vowels would still be present in the spoken form of the language. An English translation of Deut 6:5 serves as an example: "Love Yahweh your God with all your heart." Without the vowels, we are left with a series of consonants, much like the ancient written form of Hebrew: *Lv Yahweh yr Gd wth ll yr hrt.* In order to read this sentence out loud, you would need to rely on your knowledge of English and supply the necessary vowels. In the same way, when Joshua read the entire law of Moses to the Israelites (Josh 8), he had before him a consonantal text with no vowels. This required Joshua to supply from memory the necessary vowels when reading. Fortunately for us, this is not the case today.

In the second half of the first millennium AD, a group of dedicated scribes called Masoretes became concerned with preserving the oral tradition or spoken form of Hebrew. They developed an elaborate system of vowel notation called pointing. This pointing system was designed to preserve the spoken system of vowels in the written text. Because the Masoretic scribes considered the biblical text to be sacred, their vowel symbols were designed in such a way as never to interfere with the original consonantal text. To do this, the Masoretes developed vowel symbols that were placed under, inside (to the left), or above the consonantal characters. Let us look once again at Deut 6:5, but this time in Hebrew. You will notice that the consonants are exactly the same in both examples. In the second example, however, the Masoretic vowel pointing system has been added. Notice that the majority of symbols exist below the consonants with occasional symbols inside (to the left) or over the consonants.

| | Deut 6:5 |
|---|---|
| without vowels | ואהבת את יהוה אלהיך בכל לבבך |
| with vowels | וְאָהַבְתָּ אֵת יְהוָה אֱלֹהֶיךָ בְּכָל לְבָבְךָ |

## 2.2 Hebrew Vowel Charts

In the following charts, Hebrew vowels are presented in three major categories: long, short, and reduced. Within these vowel types, there are five vowel classes (a, e, i, o, u), though not all classes are attested in each of the vowel types. Each vowel will appear with the consonant בּ in order to show the position of the vowel with respect to its consonant. It is important to note that the vowel is pronounced after the consonant with which it is associated. For example, בַּ is pronounced *ba* and not *ab*, and בֹּ is pronounced *bo* and not *ob*.

For each vowel, you should be able to give its vowel class, vowel type, and vowel name. For now, do not be concerned about the physical similarity between the Qamets (בָּ, long a) and the Qamets Hatuf (בָּ, short o). You will learn how to distinguish between them in the next chapter. Finally, you should begin the process of becoming familiar with the pronunciation of each vowel. Learning the sound for each vowel will take some time. Be patient.

## 2.3 Hebrew Vowel Chart 1: Long Vowels

In this type, i-class and u-class vowels are not attested. These vowels are often called "changeable" long vowels.

## 2.7 Hebrew Vowel Letters

In the charts below, you will encounter what are called "vowel letters." The ancient Hebrew scribe was well aware of the difficulties associated with reading texts without vowels. To this end, as early as the tenth century BC the innovative scribes would occasionally use certain Hebrew consonants to indicate different vowel sounds. The consonant ה was generally used to indicate the a vowels (but others also). The consonant י was used to indicate the i and e vowels, and the consonant ו was used to indicate u and o vowels. A good example of this phenomenon is the variable spelling of David's name. Originally it was spelled דּוד. But later, it was spelled דּויד. In the second example, you will notice the insertion of י to indicate the i vowel in the second half of the name. The י is not functioning as a consonant but as a vowel. In other words, certain Hebrew letters can be either consonantal or vocalic. In general, vowel letters are written with a combination of a vowel (long or short) and a consonant (ה, ו, or י). The most common vowel letters are Hireq Yod (ִי), Holem Waw (וֹ) and Shureq (וּ).

## 2.8 Vowel Letters Written with ה

Note that ה is used as a vowel letter only at the end of a word, as in תּוֹרָה (law) and יִבְנֶה (he will build). The name of each is a combination of the name of the vowel (e.g., "Qamets") and the name of the consonant (e.g., "He").

| Class | Symbol | Vowel Name | Pronunciation |
|---|---|---|---|
| a-class | בָּה | Qamets He | a as in father |
| e-class | בֵּה | Tsere He | e as in they |
| e-class | בֶּה | Seghol He | e as in better |
| o-class | בֹּה | Holem He | o as in role |

## 2.9 Vowel Letters Written with ו

These vowels are often referred to as unchangeable long vowels because they do not reduce or "change." Note that the name of the u-class vowel in this type is unique. Its name is not a combination of any vowel or consonant as is the case with all other vowel letters.

| Class | Symbol | Vowel Name | Pronunciation |
|---|---|---|---|
| o-class | בּוֹ | Holem Waw | o as in role |
| u-class | בּוּ | Shureq | u as in ruler |

## 2.10 Vowel Letters Written with י

These vowels are also referred to as unchangeable long vowels because they do not reduce or "change." The name of each is a combination of the name of the vowel (e.g., "Hireq") and the name of the consonant (e.g., "Yod"). Some grammarians consider Qamets Yod and Pathach Yod to be vowel letters; others consider them to be diphthongs (see 3.10).

| Class | Symbol | Vowel Name | Pronunciation |
|---|---|---|---|
| e-class | בֵּי | Tsere Yod | e as in they |
| e-class | בֶּי | Seghol Yod | e as in better |
| i-class | בִּי | Hireq Yod | i as in machine |

## 2.12 Defective Writing

You have just learned that certain vowels use a consonant in the formation of their symbol and that these vowels are called vowel letters. Sometimes, certain vowel letters are written without their consonant. This phenomenon, known as defective writing, occurs with some frequency. It should also be noted that when these vowels are written with their consonants it is called full writing. The full or defective writing of a word makes no difference in the meaning of the word. There are three patterns of defective writing to be learned.

| Full Writing | Defective Writing | Change | Example (full → defective) | Gloss |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| בּוֹ | בֹּ | Holem Waw → Holem | שׁוֹפָר → שֹׁפָר | ram's horn |
| בּוּ | בֻּ | Shureq → Qibbuts | מַדּוּעַ → מַדֻּעַ | why? |
| בִּי | בִּ | Hireq Yod → Hireq | דָּוִיד → דָּוִד | David |

There is no certain way to predict when a vowel will be written defectively. Familiarity with vocabulary and frequent exposure to this phenomenon will help you in the identification of defectively written words.

## 2.13 Shewa

You will notice in Hebrew Vowel Chart 3 (2.5) that each reduced vowel (בֲּ, בֱּ, בֳּ) exhibits a pair of vertical dots on the right side of the vowel symbol. These vertical dots also occur without an accompanying vowel (בְּ). This vowel sign is called Shewa. The Shewa is not listed in the above vowel charts because it is not like any other vowel. The Shewa does not belong to any vowel class (a, e, i, o, u), nor does it belong to any vowel type (long, short, reduced). There are two basic types of Shewa in Hebrew: Silent Shewa and Vocal Shewa. The Silent Shewa has a zero value and is never pronounced. The Vocal Shewa maintains a hurried pronunciation and sounds like the *a* in *amuse*. In the next chapter, you will learn how to distinguish between Silent and Vocal Shewa.

## 2.14 Holem over the שׂ and שׁ

In some instances, when the vowel symbol Holem (בֹּ) is used with שׂ (Sin) or שׁ (Shin), the two dots combine into one dot over the consonant. For example, we would expect to see something like יֹשְׁבִים with two distinct dots. But the two dots will sometimes combine into a single dot (ישְׁבִים) that will serve two purposes. It distinguishes between שׁ and שׂ, and it also serves as the long o-class vowel (Holem). You will learn in the next chapter that all syllable initial consonants must be followed by a vowel. Given this rule, the dot over the שׁ in the above example must also function as the vowel Holem. For a different variation, the dot over the Sin in שׂנֵא distinguishes between שׁ and שׂ and it also serves as the vowel Holem.

## 2.15 Daghesh Forte

There is one more pointing symbol that requires discussion, the Daghesh Forte. You know its twin, the Daghesh Lene. In chapter 1, you learned that six Hebrew consonants (the begadkephat letters) have two possible pronunciations. In order to distinguish between the two pronunciations, a small dot (Daghesh Lene) was inserted into the consonant (בּ for the hard b and ב for the soft v). The Daghesh Forte looks exactly like the Daghesh Lene. The effect of the Daghesh Forte, however, is to double the consonant in which it occurs. For example, the Hebrew word הַשָּׁמַיִם (the heavens) has a Daghesh Forte located in the Shin (שּׁ). Technically, this means the word has two Shins. The Daghesh Forte can occur in any consonant except the gutturals (א, ה, ח, ע) and ר. The gutturals and ר, therefore, will never take a Daghesh Lene or Forte. Like the Daghesh Lene, the Daghesh Forte can occur in a begadkephat consonant. It is important to note that when a Daghesh Forte occurs in a begadkephat consonant, it doubles the hard pronunciation and not the soft. In the next chapter, you will learn how to distinguish between the Daghesh Lene and the Daghesh Forte when they appear in begadkephat letters.

## 2.16 Summary

Make sure that you master the information in the summary below before moving on to the exercises.

1. Long, short, and reduced vowels are summarized in 2.6. Memorize the form, pronunciation, and name for each vowel symbol. You should also be able to give its vowel class (a, e, i, o, u) and vowel type (long, short, reduced).
2. Vowel letters are summarized in 2.11. Memorize the form, pronunciation, and name for each vowel letter. You should also be able to give its vowel class (a, e, i, o, u).
3. Vowel letters written with ה (He) occur only at the end of a word, as in תּוֹרָה (law) and יִבְנֶה (he will build).
4. Vowel letters written with ו (Waw) and י (Yod) are often referred to as unchangeable long vowels because, as you will learn later, they do not reduce or "change."
5. Defective writing is that phenomenon in which certain vowel letters are written without their consonant. There are three patterns of defective writing to be learned: Holem Waw → Holem; Shureq → Qibbuts; Hireq Yod → Hireq.
6. The Daghesh Forte (בּ) doubles the consonant in which it appears. It can occur in any consonant except the gutturals and ר.

## 2.18 Additional Information: Alternative Vowel Classification

Originally, there were only three vowel classes in Hebrew: a-class, i-class, and u-class. The other vowel classes (e-class and o-class) developed later. The e-class developed from the original i-class and the o-class developed from the original u-class. For this reason, most grammarians prefer to divide Hebrew vowels into three (a, i, u) rather than five (a, e, i, o, u) classes. By doing this, e-class vowels are considered to be a subset of the i-class, and o-class vowels are considered to be a subset of the u-class. Other grammarians, however, divide Hebrew vowels into the five classes with which most students are familiar from their study of English. In this grammar, we have adopted the five-class system. For your reference, the following chart shows both categorization systems together. Note that this information is not presented for memorization but so that you might be familiar with other grammar presentations.

| | a-class | i-class (incl. e-class) | u-class (incl. o-class) |
|---|---|---|---|
| | **a** | **e** | **i** | **o** | **u** |
| Long | בָּ Qamets | בֵּ Tsere | — | בֹּ Holem | — |
| Short | בַּ Pathach | בֶּ Seghol | בִּ Hireq | בָּ Qamets Hatuf | בֻּ Qibbuts |
| Reduced | בֲּ Hateph Pathach | בֱּ Hateph Seghol | — | בֳּ Hateph Qamets | — |

## 2.19 Something You Should Know: The Spelling of the Divine Name

In the vocabulary section for this chapter, twenty-one of the most frequently occurring proper nouns in the Hebrew Bible are listed. You will observe that one name stands out from all of the other names. It is the personal name for God in the Old Testament, the divine name יהוה (Yahweh, the LORD). In scholarly literature, the divine name is often referred to as the tetragrammaton, from the Greek meaning "four letters." The statistics on the divine name reveal its importance. It is by far the most frequent proper noun in the Hebrew Bible with 6,828 occurrences. You will also observe that this is the only proper noun in the vocabulary list that does not appear with vowel pointing. When it appears in the Hebrew Bible, however, the divine name is spelled as either יְהוָה or יְהוִה. But as you can see, the vowels do not actually correspond to the consonants of the tetragrammaton. Why these vowels and what do they represent?

Over time, in Hebrew and rabbinic traditions, there was concern not to misuse the divine name or to "take the name of Yahweh your God in vain" (Exod 20:7; Deut 5:11). So the practice arose of saying אֲדֹנָי (adonai, "Lord") whenever יהוה occurred in the text. In order to remind readers of this reality, the vowels for אֲדֹנָי (adonai) were applied to יהוה as יְהוָה. The Hateph Pathach under the א of אֲדֹנָי became a Vocal Shewa under the non-guttural י and the Holem following ד was normally left out.

In addition to יְהוָה, the divine name also appears as יְהוִה or יְהֹוִה. In this case, the vowels indicate that readers should pronounce יהוה as אֱלֹהִים (elohim, God). Once again, the Hateph Seghol under the א of אֱלֹהִים became Vocal Shewa under the non-guttural י and the Holem following ל was normally left out. This spelling was used when the title אֲדֹנָי (adonai, "Lord") appeared together with the divine name, as in אֲדֹנָי יְהוִה. This prevented a reader from pronouncing *adonai adonai*, a repetition that would confuse the appearance of the personal name with the title "Lord." And so, to distinguish between the two different words used for God in this construction, readers would say, *adonai elohim*.

In desiring to show reverence for the divine name, avoiding its pronunciation became the standard practice, and this is still the tradition of the Jewish community today. The archaic word Jehovah represents the German transliteration of the tetragrammaton with the vowels for אֲדֹנָי (adonai). Contemporary scholarship is in general agreement, however, that based on how the Hebrew vowel system operates, the original pronunciation was likely "Yahweh." This rendering of the divine name is typically employed in biblical studies and the Christian church today. Regardless of how one spells or pronounces the divine name, either in the past or in the present, it is important to understand the significance of God's name. As it is written, "This is my name forever, the name by which I am to be remembered from generation to generation" (Exod 3:15). Because of its significance, the meaning of the divine name is further discussed in 24.18, 25.15, 26.17, and 27.19.
