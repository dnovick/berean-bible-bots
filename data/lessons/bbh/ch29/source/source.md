# BBH Chapter 29 — Hophal Weak Verbs

*Source: Pratico & Van Pelt, Basics of Biblical Hebrew Grammar (BBH), Chapter 29*

---

## 29.1 Introduction (§29.1)

Weak verb forms of the Hophal occur far more frequently than strong verb forms — approximately 250 weak verbs compared to only 50 strong verbs. Master the strong verb diagnostics (Ch28) before studying the weak verb variations. Weak verbs in the Hophal prefer either **o-class** or **u-class** vowels with the various prefixes and preformatives. As with the strong verb, only the **Perfect, Imperfect, and Participle** of selected weak verbs are studied.

## 29.3 I-Guttural Notes (§29.3)

1. I-Guttural verbs in the Hophal prefer the **Qamets Hatuf** (o-class) prefix or preformative vowel. However, Qibbuts (u-class) is also attested (7 times).
2. I-Guttural verbs maintain all strong verb diagnostics. A **reduced vowel (Hateph Qamets)** under the guttural in first root position is expected, consistent with I-Guttural patterns in other stems (§§16.2–3).

## 29.5 III-ה Notes (§29.5)

1. Like I-Guttural verbs, III-ה verbs in the Hophal prefer the **o-class** (Qamets Hatuf) prefix or preformative vowel.
2. The final ה of the verbal root is dropped in the Perfect, Imperfect, and Participle. Where a final ה does appear, it is a **vowel letter**, not the final root consonant.

## 29.7 I-נ Notes (§29.7)

1. I-נ Hophal verbs prefer the **u-class** prefix or preformative vowel (Qibbuts).
2. The נ in first root position assimilates into the second root consonant, remaining as a **Daghesh Forte** (הֻצַּל). With only two root consonants visible, the Daghesh Forte is the clue for identifying the I-נ verbal root.

## 29.9 I-י Notes (§29.9)

The last three weak verb classes in the Hophal (I-י, Biconsonantal, and Geminate) each prefer a **u-class vowel** — frequently **Shureq**, sometimes Qibbuts.

1. In the I-י paradigms, Shureq (וּ) appears as the prefix or preformative vowel in each conjugation.
2. Most I-י verbs were originally I-ו. In the Hophal stem, the original ו reappears as the vowel letter **Shureq** (וּ) in the Perfect (הוּשַׁב), Imperfect (יוּשַׁב), and Participle (מוּשָׁב). With only two root consonants remaining, the Shureq is diagnostic of the I-י class. The original ו also reappears in the Niphal (§§25.10–11) and Hiphil (§§27.12–13) stems.

## 29.11 Biconsonantal Notes (§29.11)

1. **Shureq** prefix or preformative vowel in every form of the Perfect, Imperfect, and Participle.
2. The medial vowel letters of the Biconsonantal verb classes do not appear in the Hophal stem. The Perfect and Imperfect have a **Pathach stem vowel**, except in forms with sufformatives that begin with or consist of a vowel. The Participle has a **Qamets stem vowel**, except in the feminine singular.

## 29.13 Geminate Notes (§29.13)

1. **Shureq** prefix or preformative vowel in every form, though some Geminate forms show a Qibbuts prefix vowel.
2. Only two root consonants are present because only one Geminate consonant is preserved. In some forms, the **Daghesh Forte** in the remaining Geminate consonant aids root identification. The **Holem Waw connecting vowel** in the Perfect is another indicator of the Geminate class (§§14.8–9).

---

## 29.14 Summary (§29.14)

Five weak verb classes preserve only two root consonants:
- **III-ה**: final ה is lost
- **I-נ**: נ assimilates into the second root consonant as Daghesh Forte
- **I-י** (originally I-ו): vowel letter Shureq replaces the י as prefix or preformative vowel
- **Geminate**: only one Geminate consonant preserved, represented as Daghesh Forte in some forms
- **Biconsonantal**: both root consonants remain; Shureq is the prefix or preformative vowel in every form

**The Hophal Stem — Weak Verb Summary:**

| Verb Class | Perfect | Imperfect | Participle |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Strong (u-class)** | הֻקְטַל | יֻקְטַל | מֻקְטָל |
| **Strong (o-class)** | הָקְטַל | יָקְטַל | מָקְטָל |
| **I-Guttural** | הָעֳמַד | יָעֳמַד | מָעֳמָד |

## 29.16 Additional Information: Types of Hebrew Clauses (§29.16)

### 1 — Conditional Clause

A conditional sentence has two parts: the **protasis** (if-clause, stating the condition) and the **apodosis** (then-clause, stating the consequence). The protasis often begins with אִם (if).

| Clause Type | Example |
|---|---|
| Protasis | אִם תֵּלְכִי עִמִּי ("if you will go with me") |
| Apodosis | וְהָלָכְתִּי ("then I will go") |

> אִם־תֵּלְכִי עִמִּי וְהָלָכְתִּי וְאִם־לֹא תֵלְכִי עִמִּי לֹא אֵלֵךְ
> Barak said to her, "If you will go with me, then I will go; but if you will not go with me, then I will not go" (Judg 4:8).

In addition to אִם, the protasis may begin with כִּי, הֵן, or אֲשֶׁר followed by a Perfect, Imperfect, or Participle. The apodosis often begins with וְ.

> כִּי־תַעֲלֶה בָבֶל הַשָּׁמַיִם… מֵאִתִּי יָבֹאוּ שֹׁדְדִים לָהּ
> If Babylon were to reach up to heaven… destroyers would come from me upon her (Jer 51:53).

> וָמֵת וְעָזַב אֶת־אָבִיו
> If he leaves his father, then he (his father) will die (Gen 44:22).

---

### 2 — Causal Clause

A causal clause indicates what causes another clause to happen. Causal clauses often begin with כִּי (because, for) or אֲשֶׁר (because, for).

> כִּי־הָאִישׁ אָמַר אֵלֵינוּ לֹא נֵרֵד
> We will not go down because the man spoke to us (Gen 43:5).

---

### 3 — Purpose Clause

A purpose clause states the goal the subject hopes to achieve. Purpose clauses often begin with לִמַ֫עַן (so that), בַּעֲבוּר (so that), אֲשֶׁר (so that), לְבִלְתִּי (so that … not), לְמַ֫עַן לֹא (so that not), or פֶּן (lest).

> יִיטַב לְךָ אֲשֶׁר וְשָׁמַרְתָּ לַעֲשׂוֹת
> And you will be careful to do so that it will go well with you (Deut 6:3).

> פֶּן־נָפוּץ עַל־פְּנֵי כָל־הָאָרֶץ נַעֲשֶׂה־לָּנוּ שֵׁם
> Let us make a name for ourselves lest we become scattered over the face of all the earth (Gen 11:4).

---

### 4 — Result Clause

A result clause states something that is true or happens because of another clause. Result clauses often begin with a Waw Consecutive verb or with כִּי (so, that).

> אֵלֵךְ אֶל־פַּרְעֹה כִּי מִי אָנֹכִי
> Who am I that I can go to Pharaoh (Exod 3:11)!

---

### 5 — Concessive Clause

A concessive clause states something that might prevent what is stated in another clause but in fact does not. Concessive clauses are often marked with אִם, גַּם, גַּם כִּי, וְ, כִּי, or עַל, all translatable as "although" or "even though."

> גַּם כִּי־אֵלֵךְ בְּגֵיא צַלְמָוֶת לֹא־אִירָא רָע
> Even though I walk through the valley of the shadow of death, I will not fear evil (Ps 23:4).

> וְאָנֹכִי נַעַר קָטֹן אַתָּה הִמְלַכְתָּ אֶת־עַבְדְּךָ תַּחַת דָּוִד אָבִי
> You have made your servant king in place of David my father, even though I am a very young man (1 Kgs 3:7).

## 29.17 Something You Should Know: But By My Name (§29.17)

> Few verses in the Old Testament have generated the scholarly attention received by Exod 6:2–3.
>
> וַיְדַבֵּר אֱלֹהִים אֶל־מֹשֶׁה וַיֹּאמֶר אֵלָיו אֲנִי יְהוָה וָאֵרָא אֶל־אַבְרָהָם אֶל־יִצְחָק וְאֶל־יַעֲקֹב בְּאֵל שַׁדַָּי וּשְׁמִי יְהוָה לֹא נוֹדַעְתִּי לָהֶם
> God said to Moses, "I am Yahweh. I appeared to Abraham, to Isaac, and to Jacob as El Shaddai, but by my name Yahweh I did not make myself known to them."
>
> Based on these verses, many scholars deny to the patriarchs any knowledge of the divine name יהוה, contrary to its appearance nearly 170 times in Genesis and the first three chapters of Exodus. It is argued, nevertheless, that the patriarchal fathers worshiped El Shaddai and had no knowledge of the name יהוה.
>
> Contrary to this view is the interpretation that the fathers knew the name but did not understand the fullness of its revelation. Exod 6:2–8 is not a new revelation of the name; it is a **nuanced revelation** of the name. The name is now being revealed afresh in the setting of God's dynamic and miraculous intrusion into human history for the primary purpose of redeeming his people from Egypt. Understanding the use of the noun שֵׁם (name) as detailed earlier (§24.18), Exod 6:2 may be interpretively translated as follows:
>
> I appeared to Abraham, to Isaac, and to Jacob as El Shaddai,
> but in the character expressed by my name Yahweh
> I did not make myself known to them.
>
> With regard to the etymology of the tetragrammaton (יהוה), there is general agreement that it derives from the verb "to be" (הוה, originally הוה) and is associated with an Imperfect 3ms form. An analysis of historical and textual context will contribute more to understanding the name than etymological analysis alone. The context of Exod 3:14–15 would appear to favor the Qal derivation. The name is not metaphysical but **dynamic**. Mightily and miraculously (Exod 7:3), the Lord intervenes in time and space to effect his redemptive purposes for his covenant people. Exod 3:13–15 and 6:2–8 record a new stage in the progressive self-revelation of God's name and character to his people. One cannot help but recall that in the sacramental setting of Jesus' last meal with his disciples, he said, "I have manifested your name" (John 17:6). Implicit in "your name" is the person, character, authority, and redemptive work of God.
>
