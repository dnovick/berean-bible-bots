# BBH Chapter 23 — Sentence Syntax

*Source: Pratico & Van Pelt, Basics of Biblical Hebrew Grammar (BBH), Chapter 23*

---

## 23.1 Introduction (§23.1)

Before moving on to study the derived stems, we pause to treat word order in verbal sentences along with a selection of other issues relating to sentence and verbal syntax. The goal is to organize and clarify many issues of syntax from earlier chapters in the grammar.

In English, normal word order is subject-verb-object. In the sentence "Jacob loved Rachel," Jacob is the subject because his name precedes the verb, and Rachel is the object because her name follows the verb. In Hebrew, word order for a verbal sentence is often characterized as verb-subject-object. The following examples illustrate this basic guideline:

> וַיִּזְכֹּר אֱלֹהִים אֶת־נֹחַ
> (And) God remembered Noah (Gen 8:1).

> וַיַּחֲלֹם יֹוסֵף חֲלֹום
> (And) Joseph dreamed a dream (Gen 37:5).

In the above examples, the verbs are in first position, the subjects are in second position, and the objects are last (verb-subject-object). Variations from this pattern are common. Additionally, certain particles, prepositional phrases, or other sentence components often occur in the midst of the basic word order.

---

## 23.2 The Syntax of the Verb (§23.2)

In the syntax of a verbal sentence, the verb normally appears at the beginning of the clause in which it occurs.

1. In the following examples, the verb appears first in its clause:

> מֶלֶךְ־חָדָשׁ עַל־מִצְרָיִם וַיָּקָם
> (And) a new king arose over Egypt (Exod 1:8).

> לַיהוָה שִׁיר חָדָשׁ שִׁירוּ
> Sing to Yahweh a new song (Ps 96:1).

2. There are instances, however, when the verb will not appear at the beginning of the clause:

> לְעֹלָם וָעֶד יִמְלֹךְ יְהוָה
> Yahweh will reign forever and ever (Exod 15:18).

> לָכֶם אֶת־הַשִּׁירָה הַזֹּאת כִּתְבוּ וְעַתָּה
> And now, write down this song for yourselves (Deut 31:19).

---

## 23.3 The Syntax of the Subject (§23.3)

Perfect, Imperfect, and Imperative verbs have an implied subject. This means that you will not always need an explicit subject to complete a sentence in Hebrew. When you do have an explicit subject, it may appear either before or after the verb.

1. In the syntax of the verbal sentence, the verb is normally followed by its subject if one is specified:

> אֶל־הָאִשָּׁה הַנָּחָשׁ וַיֹּאמֶר
> The serpent said to the woman (Gen 3:4).

> כַּעֲפַר הָאָרֶץ זַרְעֲךָ וְהָיָה
> Your offspring (seed) will be like the dust of the earth (Exod 24:16).

2. However, since some verbs prefer not to appear in first position, an explicit verbal subject may appear before the verb:

> יִמְלֹךְ לְעֹלָם וָעֶד יְהוָה
> Yahweh will reign forever and ever (Exod 15:18).

> מָצָא חֵן בְּעֵינֵי יְהוָה וְנֹחַ
> And Noah found favor in the eyes of Yahweh (Gen 6:8).

---

## 23.4 The Syntax of the Object (§23.4)

There are a number of different types of "objects" in Hebrew. The most important are the indirect (dative) and the direct (accusative) objects.

1. The indirect object is the person or thing indirectly affected by the action of the verb. It is usually marked with the preposition לְ (to, for) or אֶל־ (to, for) and may either precede or follow the direct object (if any):

> אֶת־הָאָרֶץ לָכֶם יָדַעְתִּי כִּי־נָתַן יְהוָה
> I know that Yahweh has given to you the land (Josh 2:9).

> אֶל־כָּל־יִשְׂרָאֵל וַיִּקְרָא מֹשֶׁה
> (And) Moses called to all Israel (Deut 5:1).

2. A direct object (if any) will frequently appear immediately after the verb or its subject. It may also precede or follow an indirect object:

> אֵת הַשָּׁמַיִם וְאֵת הָאָרֶץ בָּרָא אֱלֹהִים
> God created the heavens and the earth (Gen 1:1).

> מִשְׁאֲלֹת לִבֶּךָ וְיִתֶּן־לְךָ
> (And) he will give to you the desires of your heart (Ps 37:4).

3. The direct object may also appear before the verb, at the beginning of the clause:

> תִּירָא אֶת־יְהוָה אֱלֹהֶיךָ
> Yahweh your God you shall fear (Deut 10:20).

> יִשְׁפֹּט הָאֱלֹהִים אֶת־הַצַּדִּיק וְאֶת־הָרָשָׁע
> The righteous and the wicked God will judge (Eccl 3:17).

---

## 23.5 Additional Clause Components (§23.5)

While word order in a verbal clause is normally characterized as verb-subject-object, subjects and objects may appear before the verb in important contexts. Additionally, a verb may also be preceded by an adverb or adverbial phrase, by the word הִנֵּה (behold), by an expression that provides circumstantial information, by an independent personal pronoun, or by a negative particle.

1. The verb may be preceded by an adverb:

> יָדַעְתִּי כִּי־גָדוֹל יְהוָה מִכָּל־הָאֱלֹהִים עַתָּה
> Now I know that Yahweh is greater than all gods (Exod 18:11).

2. The verb may be preceded by an expression that provides context, circumstantial information, or a connection with a preceding clause:

> יַעֲזָב־אִישׁ אֶת־אָבִיו וְאֶת־אִמּוֹ עַל־כֵּן
> Therefore, a man shall leave his father and his mother (Gen 2:24).

3. The verb may be preceded by הִנֵּה (behold):

> נָפְלוּ אֲבוֹתֵינוּ בֶּחָרֶב וְהִנֵּה
> And behold, our fathers have fallen by the sword (2 Chr 29:9).

4. The verb may be preceded by a negative particle:

> לֹא־אִירָא רָע
> I will not fear evil (Ps 23:4).

5. The verb may be preceded by an independent personal pronoun:

> תִּהְיוּ־לִי מַמְלֶכֶת כֹּהֲנִים וְאַתֶּם
> And you will be to me a kingdom of priests (Exod 19:6).

6. The verb may be preceded by a number of other modifiers, particles, or grammatical constructions, such as the vocatives below:

> לָמָה עֲזַבְתָּנִי אֵלִי אֵלִי
> My God, my God, why have you forsaken me? (Ps 22:2 [1])

## 23.6 Syntax of the Perfect (Suffix) Conjugation (§23.6)

Special attention to the syntax of the Perfect and Imperfect conjugations is important for at least two reasons. First, these two conjugations constitute approximately 72 percent of the verbs in the Hebrew Bible. Second, the syntax of the Perfect and Imperfect contributes to the meaning of the verb in its context. When working with these conjugations, it is important to consider the position of the verb in its clause (first or non-first) and the use or non-use of the Waw Consecutive and the regular conjunction וְ.

1. **Regular Perfect.** When a Perfect verb is used to describe a completed action, it is normally not the first word in its clause, though there are some exceptions:

> יְהוָה אֶת־אַבְרָם בְּרִית כָּרַת בַּיּוֹם הַהוּא
> In that day, Yahweh made (cut) a covenant with Abram (Gen 15:18).

> בְּרֵאשִׁית בָּרָא אֱלֹהִים אֵת הַשָּׁמַיִם וְאֵת הָאָרֶץ
> In the beginning, God created the heavens and the earth (Gen 1:1).

2. **Consecutive Perfect.** When a Perfect verb is prefixed with the Waw Consecutive, it will be translated with the values of the Imperfect. The consecutive Perfect is normally used for future tense narrative sequences (and also to express habitual-durative action) and appears as the first word in its clause:

> אֵת יְהוָה אֱלֹהֶיךָ וְאָהַבְתָּ
> And you will love Yahweh your God (Deut 6:5).

> אֶת־בְּרִיתִי וְזָכַרְתִּי
> And I will remember my covenant (Gen 9:15).

3. **Imperative-Consecutive Perfect Sequence.** An Imperative may be followed by one or more consecutive Perfects. In this sequence, the Perfect verb may carry the full force of the preceding Imperative in its translation:

> בָהָר לִפְנֵי יְהוָה צֵא וְעָמַדְתָּ וַיֹּאמֶר
> And he said, "Go out and stand on the mountain before Yahweh" (1 Kgs 19:11).

> אֲלֵהֶם וְאָמַרְתָּ אֶת־זִקְנֵי יִשְׂרָאֵל לֵךְ וְאָסַפְתָּ
> Go and assemble the elders of Israel and say to them … (Exod 3:16).

---

## 23.7 Syntax of the Imperfect (Prefix) Conjugation (§23.7)

As demonstrated above, the syntax of the Perfect conjugation contributes to the meaning of the verb in its context. This is also true for the Imperfect conjugation. When working with the Imperfect (and Perfect) conjugation, it is important to consider the position of the verb in its clause (first or non-first) and the use or non-use of the Waw Consecutive and the regular conjunction וְ.

1. **Regular Imperfect.** When an Imperfect verb is used to describe an incomplete action, it is normally not the first word in its clause, though there are some exceptions:

> עָקֵב תְּשׁוּפֶנּוּ רֹאשׁ וְאַתָּה יְשׁוּפְךָ הוּא
> He will crush you on the head and you will crush him on the heel (Gen 3:15).

> לְעֹלָם וָעֶד יִמְלֹךְ יְהוָה
> Yahweh will reign forever and ever (Exod 15:18).

2. **Consecutive Imperfect.** When an Imperfect verb is prefixed with the Waw Consecutive, it is normally translated as a simple past tense in English. The consecutive Imperfect is commonly used for a past tense narrative sequence and always appears as the first word in its clause:

> יְהוּדָה אִשָּׁה לְעֵר בְּכוֹרוֹ וַיִּקַּח
> And Judah took a wife for Er, his firstborn (Gen 38:6).

> שְׁמוֹ רְאוּבֵן וַתִּקְרָא בֵּן וַתֵּלֶד לֵאָה וַתַּהַר
> And Leah conceived and she bore a son and she called his name Reuben (Gen 29:32).

3. **Volitional Imperfect.** The volitional Imperfect is used to express a wish, request, or command as in, "Let us make man" or "May Yahweh forgive!" When translating the volitional Imperfect, use the key words "let" or "may." The particle נָא may also occur with the volitional Imperfect. The volitional Imperfect is normally the first word in its clause, though there are some exceptions:

> יְהוָה לְעַבְדְּךָ יִסְלַח־נָא
> May Yahweh forgive your servant (2 Kgs 5:18).

> אָדָם בְּצַלְמֵנוּ נַעֲשֶׂה
> Let us make man in our image (Gen 1:26).

4. **Imperative-Imperfect Sequence.** An Imperative verb may be followed by an Imperfect verb, a construction that will create a purpose or result clause. In this sequence, the Imperfect will be marked with the conjunction וְ (not Waw Consecutive) and translated as "so that":

> וְנִחְיֶה וְשִׁבְרוּ־לָנוּ מִשָּׁם רְדוּ־שָׁמָּה
> Go down there and buy grain for us from there so that we might live (Gen 42:2).

> אֹתְךָ אֶל־הַמֶּלֶךְ וְאֶשְׁלְחָה הֵנָּה בֹּא
> Come here so that I may send you to the king (2 Sam 14:32).

## 23.8 Verbless Clauses in Biblical Hebrew (§23.8)

A clause contains a subject and a predicate. Hebrew is capable of indicating predication without an explicit verb, with the so-called verbless clause.

1. **Verbless Clause Components.** In Hebrew, a verbless clause consists of a subject and a noun or its equivalent serving as the predicate. The subject and predicate appear together (side by side) and may consist of a noun, adjective, independent personal pronoun, demonstrative pronoun, interrogative pronoun, prepositional phrase, or participle. In English, a form of the verb "to be" is used to translate the predication:

> שָׁמַיִם לַיהוָה הַשָּׁמַיִם
> The heavens are heavens belonging to Yahweh (Ps 115:16).

> כְּבוֹדוֹ גָּדוֹל
> His glory is great (Ps 21:6[5]).

> מָגֵן לָךְ אָנֹכִי
> I am your shield (Gen 15:1).

> הַבְּרִית זֹאת
> This is the covenant (Jer 31:33).

> מִי־הָאִישׁ הֶחָכָם
> Who is the wise man? (Jer 9:11[12])

> עַל־פְּנֵי תְהוֹם וְחֹשֶׁךְ
> And darkness was over the face of the deep (Gen 1:2).

> עַל־כִּסֵּא מַלְכוּתוֹ יוֹשֵׁב וְהַמֶּלֶךְ
> And the king was sitting on the throne of his kingdom (Esth 5:1).

2. **Word Order and Types of Verbless Clauses.** In Hebrew, the word order for a verbless clause is variable, but this variation is often meaningful.

   If the word order is **subject-predicate**, it may be understood as a verbless clause of **identification** — indicating "who" or "what" is the subject:

   > מָגֵן לָךְ אָנֹכִי
   > I am your shield (Gen 15:1).

   > הַבְּרִית זֹאת
   > This is the covenant (Jer 31:33).

   If the word order is **predicate-subject**, it may be understood as a verbless clause of **classification** — indicating the general class to which the subject belongs:

   > כְּבוֹדוֹ גָּדוֹל
   > His glory is great (Ps 21:6[5]).

   > אַתָּה עָפָר
   > You are dust (Gen 3:19).

   In English, the preferred order for translation is subject-predicate, regardless of the word order in Hebrew. Though there are exceptions to these patterns, this is a helpful starting point.

3. **Verbless Clauses with a Third Person Independent Personal Pronoun.** A third person independent personal pronoun may appear in a verbless clause as an indicator of predication. In this usage, the pronoun is not translated:

   > הַהֹלֵךְ לְפָנֶיךָ הוּא וַיהוָה
   > Yahweh is the one who goes before you (Deut 31:8).

   > אוֹצָרוֹ הִיא יִרְאַת יְהוָה
   > The fear of Yahweh is his treasure (Isa 33:6).

   > מַלְכִּי אֱלֹהִים אַתָּה־הוּא
   > You are my king, O God (Ps 44:5[4]).

---

## 23.9 Summary (§23.9)

1. In Hebrew, word order for a verbal sentence is normally verb-subject-object. However, subjects and objects may appear before the verb in important contexts. Additionally, a verb may also be preceded by an adverb of time, an adverbial phrase, the word הִנֵּה (behold), an expression that provides circumstantial information, an independent personal pronoun, or a negative particle.
2. When working with the Perfect and Imperfect conjugations, it is important to consider the position of the verb in its clause (first or non-first) and the use or non-use of the Waw Consecutive and the regular conjunction וְ.
3. The regular Perfect does not normally appear first in its clause. The consecutive Perfect appears first in its clause. When following an Imperative verb, the consecutive Perfect may carry the full force of the preceding Imperative.
4. The regular Imperfect does not normally appear first in its clause. The consecutive Imperfect appears first in its clause. The volitional Imperfect appears first in its clause but without the Waw Consecutive. When following an Imperative verb, the Imperfect (with the regular conjunction וְ) may create a purpose or result clause.
5. In Hebrew, a verbless clause consists of a subject and a noun or its equivalent serving as the predicate. The subject and predicate appear together (side by side) and may consist of a noun, adjective, independent personal pronoun, demonstrative pronoun, interrogative pronoun, prepositional phrase, or participle.

## 23.11 Additional Information: Cohortative and Jussive (§23.11)

With regard to the syntax of the Imperfect conjugation, volitional Imperfect verbs normally appear first in their clause and are used to express a wish, request, or command. When the volitional Imperfect appears in the first person (1cs, 1cp), grammars have traditionally labeled these forms as **Cohortative**. Similarly, when the volitional Imperfect appears in the third person (3ms, 3fs, 3mp, 3fp), grammars have traditionally labeled these forms as **Jussive**. Because the volitional use of the Imperfect is primarily identified by the position of the verb in its clause (not by the form of the verb itself), the designation "volitional Imperfect" is preferred for all third, second, and first person forms that express this nuance.

1. **Cohortative (first person volitional Imperfect).** The simple form of the so-called Cohortative is the same as the Imperfect 1cs and 1cp forms, אֶקְטֹל and נִקְטֹל. What identifies the volitional use of the first person Imperfect is the position of the verb in its clause. Note that what distinguishes usage in the following examples is the position of the Imperfect verb in its clause:

   > אָדָם בְּצַלְמֵנוּ נַעֲשֶׂה
   > Let us make man in our image (Gen 1:26). [volitional Imperfect — verb first]

   > נַעֲשֶׂה כֹּל אֲשֶׁר־דִּבֶּר יְהוָה
   > Everything that Yahweh has spoken we will do (Exod 19:8). [regular Imperfect — verb not first]

   While the basic first person Imperfect forms may be translated as volitional Imperfects, the so-called Cohortative is commonly spelled (approximately 60 percent of the time) with an ָה ending, as in אֶקְטְלָה (may I kill) or נִקְטְלָה (let us kill). Note that with the addition of the ָה ending, the stem vowel reduces to Vocal Shewa:

   > תּוֹרָתְךָ אֶשְׁמְרָה
   > May I observe your law (Ps 119:44).

   > בְרִית נִכְרְתָה
   > Let us make (cut) a covenant (Gen 26:28).

2. **Jussive (third person volitional Imperfect).** The basic form of the so-called Jussive is the same as the Imperfect 3ms, 3fs, 3mp, and 3fp. Once again, the Jussive or volitional use of the third person Imperfect is identified by the position of the Imperfect verb in its clause:

   > יְהוָה בֵּינִי וּבֵינֶיךָ יִשְׁפֹּט
   > May Yahweh judge between me and between you (Gen 16:5). [volitional — verb first]

   > יִשְׁפֹּט־תֵּבֵל בְּצֶדֶק וְהוּא
   > And he will judge the world with righteousness (Ps 9:9 [8]). [regular — verb not first]

   With III-ה and Biconsonantal verbs, the spelling of the volitional Imperfect 3ms may be a shortened form of the verb, just like the form of the consecutive Imperfect. All Imperfect forms below are 3ms:

| | Regular Imperfect | Consecutive Imperfect | Volitional Imperfect |
|---|---|---|---|
| | יִבְנֶה — he will build | וַיִּ֫בֶן — and he built | יִ֫בֶן — let him build |
| | יַעֲשֶׂה — he will make | וַיַּ֫עַשׂ — and he made | יַ֫עַשׂ — let him make |
| | יָקוּם — he will arise | וַיָּ֫קָם — and he arose | יָ֫קָם — let him arise |
| | יָבוֹא — he will come | וַיָּבֹא — and he came | יָבֹא — let him come |

   With III-ה and Biconsonantal 3ms verbs, the regular Imperfect forms are normally "long" forms. The consecutive and volitional Imperfect forms are normally "short" forms, though there are exceptions. In the examples that follow, the volitional Imperfect verbs are "short" and appear first in their clause. The regular Imperfect verbs are not short and do not appear first in their clause:

   > אֶת־בֵּית יְהוָה וְיִבֶן
   > And let him build the house of Yahweh (Ezra 1:3).

   > יִבְנֶה־בַּיִת לִשְׁמִי הוּא
   > He will build a house for my name (2 Sam 7:13).

   > אָבִי וְיֹאכַל מִצֵּיד בְּנוֹ יָקֻם
   > Let my father arise and let him eat from the game of his son (Gen 27:31).

   > לְעוֹלָם יָקוּם וּדְבַר־אֱלֹהֵינוּ
   > And the word of our God will stand forever (Isa 40:8).

## 23.12 Something You Should Know: Immanuel (§23.12)

> One of the most cherished names of God in the Old Testament is עִמָּנוּ אֵל ("Immanuel" or "Emmanuel"), appearing three times in Isaiah (7:14; 8:8, 10). Most readers will be familiar with the name and its meaning, but now you can understand its etymology in Hebrew. The construction עִמָּנוּ אֵל is a simple verbless clause of classification. The divine name אֵל serves as the subject of the verbless clause; עִמָּנוּ (the preposition עִם with a 1cp pronominal suffix) is the predicate. The name translates, "God is with us." The etymology of the name is easy; its theology is profound. The divine name עִמָּנוּ אֵל declares the depth of God's grace and mercy in that he gives his presence, and even himself (Matt 1:23) to his human creation.
>
> Beyond its specific context in Isa 7:14, the name עִמָּנוּ אֵל has come to symbolize and summarize the story of biblical revelation. It is a narrative through time that documents the promise of divine presence and the relentless movement of redemption, by divine initiative, from heaven to earth. This is sometimes called the "Immanuel theme" in biblical theology. The theme begins in Eden with the intimate and unhindered fellowship between God and his human creation, and it concludes with the restoration of that fellowship in the events of Rev 21:1–4. God's presence in the midst of his people to redeem and restore is a mystery, profoundly simple yet simply profound.
>
> Behold, the dwelling of God is with men. He will dwell with them, and they shall be his people, and God himself will be with them. (Rev 21:3)
>
