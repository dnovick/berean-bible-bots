# BBH Chapter 35 — The Hithpael Stem: Weak Verbs

*Source: Pratico & Van Pelt, Basics of Biblical Hebrew Grammar (BBH), Chapter 35*

---

## 35.1 Introduction

In this chapter, we will study selected weak verb classes (Geminate, III-ה, and II-Guttural) in the Hithpael stem. It is important to observe that most of the strong verb diagnostics are retained in Geminate and III-ה verbs. It is only the II-Guttural class of weak verbs that exhibits significant variation from the strong verb diagnostics. This variation is due to the fact that the guttural in second root position cannot take the Daghesh Forte of the Hithpael stem. Your study of II-Guttural verbs in the Piel (31.6–10) and Pual (33.4–8) have prepared you for this study of Hithpael II-Guttural verbs.

## 35.3 Geminate Notes

1. Most verbs in the Geminate weak verb class retain all of the Hithpael strong verb diagnostics.
2. With the Geminate verb פָּלַל (to pray), however, there is one minor exception. According to the rule of sqnmlwy (5.5, 30.15), the Daghesh Forte in the second consonant of the verbal root may be given up when this consonant is followed by Shewa, as in the Perfect 3cp (הִתְפַּלְלוּ). In light of the fact that all other strong verb diagnostics are retained, this minor variation should not present any significant difficulty.

## 35.5 III-ה Notes

1. All of the strong verb diagnostics are retained in the Hithpael forms of this weak verb class.
2. The endings that are present on these III-ה Hithpael conjugations are the same ones that were seen on the Qal and all of the other derived stems previously studied.

## 35.6 II-Guttural Verbs with Virtual Doubling: Diagnostics and Paradigms

In your study of the diagnostics for Piel and Pual II-Guttural verbs, you learned that a guttural consonant in second root position will reject the expected Daghesh Forte. The guttural's rejection of the Daghesh Forte results in either virtual doubling or compensatory lengthening (31.6, 33.4). Hithpael II-Guttural verbs respond in the same two ways. The II-Guttural verb נָחַם exhibits virtual doubling.

## 35.7 II-Guttural Verbs with Virtual Doubling: Notes

1. With the exception of the absence of the Daghesh Forte in the second consonant of the verbal root (הִתְנַחֵם), all of the other strong verb diagnostics are retained.
2. As expected, the guttural consonant in second root position rejects the Daghesh Forte of the Hithpael stem. As a result of virtual doubling, the diagnostic Pathach under the first root consonant (הִתְנַחֵם) is retained in every form of every conjugation.

---

## 35.9 II-Guttural Verbs with Compensatory Lengthening: Notes

1. In this II-Guttural (ר) paradigm with compensatory lengthening, only the distinctive prefixes and preformatives of the Hithpael strong verb diagnostics remain (נִתְ/אֶתְ/תִּתְ/יִתְ).
2. As expected, the ר in second root position rejects the Daghesh Forte of the Hithpael stem and causes the preceding Pathach to undergo compensatory lengthening. Compared to the Hithpael strong verb diagnostics, there is neither the Daghesh Forte in the second consonant of the verbal root nor is there the Pathach beneath the first root consonant. Despite these significant changes, the distinctive preformatives and prefixes in the various conjugations should make stem identification easy.

## 35.12 Additional Information: The Hithpolel Stem

The Hithpolel is the equivalent of the Hithpael stem for certain Biconsonantal (and Geminate) verbs. Like the Polel (31.13) and Polal (33.11), the Hithpolel of Biconsonantal verbs is characterized by a Holem Waw after the first consonant of the verbal root and the duplication of the second root consonant. Finally, note that the Hithpolel is distinguished from the Polel only by its distinctive prefixes (for example, הִתְ).

## 35.13 Something You Should Memorize: Psalm 66:16

The Hebrew Bible is about the character and the mighty acts of God, primarily with reference to the history of his chosen people Israel. Psalm 66 is among the most beautiful and eloquent expressions of praise for the mighty acts of God. The verse for memorization is Ps 66:16.

> לְכוּ־שִׁמְעוּ וַאֲסַפְּרָה כָּל־יִרְאֵי אֱלֹהִים אֲשֶׁר עָשָׂה לְנַפְשִׁי
>
> Come and hear, all who fear God, and I will proclaim what he has done for my soul.

The verse begins with two Qal Imperative 2ms verbs. The first Imperative (from הלךְ) serves as a particle of entreaty, joined by Maqqef to the second Imperative (from שׁמע). The verb וַאֲסַפְּרָה is a Piel Imperfect 1cs from ספר with prefixed conjunction וְ and a volitional ָה ending (Come and hear and I will proclaim). Note the spelling of the Piel verb with the volitional ָה ending (23.7.3; 23.12).

The vocative (כָּל־יִרְאֵי אֱלֹהִים) is composed of כֹּל (construct כָּל־) with Qamets Hatuf because it is connected to the following construct noun with Maqqef. The form יִרְאֵי is a Qal active Participle mp from ירא being used substantively in the construct state. The absolute noun in the construct chain is אֱלֹהִים (all who fear God).

The verse concludes with a relative clause functioning as the object of the verb (וַאֲסַפְּרָה). The relative pronoun אֲשֶׁר is followed by עָשָׂה, a Qal Perfect 3ms from עשׂה. The final form in the verse (לְנַפְשִׁי) is a prepositional phrase that consists of the Segholate noun נֶ֫פֶשׁ with a 1cs pronominal suffix and prefixed preposition לְ (what God has done for my soul).

In the verses leading up to Ps 66:16, the Psalmist speaks of the nation being "tested and refined like silver" and having gone "through fire and through water." And yet, Israel's "awesome" (נוֹרָא) God delivered them to a place of abundance through "awesome" (נוֹרָא) deeds. The mighty acts of God are no less present or apparent in the lives of God's people today than they were to his ancient people in Bible times. In fact, the ancients could never have imagined the mightiest of all God's redemptive acts (John 3:16) that should compel every believer to proclaim with joy the words of Ps 66:16, "Come and hear, all who fear God, and I will proclaim what he has done for my soul."
