# BBH Chapter 12 — Introduction to Hebrew Verbs

*Source: Pratico & Van Pelt, Basics of Biblical Hebrew Grammar (BBH), Chapter 12*

---

## 12.1 Introduction (§12.1)

The Hebrew Bible has just over 23,000 verses containing approximately 72,000 verbs — an average of three verbs per verse. Understanding the Hebrew verbal system in form, function, and meaning is essential. This chapter introduces key grammatical issues; verbal forms here are illustrative only and need not be memorized.

---

## 12.2 Verbal and Nominal Roots (§12.2)

Many nouns have verbal counterparts sharing common consonants and related meanings because they share a common **root**.

| Noun | Translation | Verb | Translation |
|---|---|---|---|
| מֶ֫לֶךְ | king | מָלַךְ | to reign |
| דָּבָר | word | דִּבֵּר | to speak |
| מִשְׁפָּט | judgment | שָׁפַט | to judge |

Hebrew roots are typically **triconsonantal** (three consonants), though a small class is biconsonantal. A root is the origin from which multiple nouns and verbs derive. For example, from root מלך come: מֶ֫לֶךְ (king), מַלְכָּה (queen), מַלְכוּת (kingdom), and מָלַךְ (to reign).

---

## 12.3 Person, Gender, and Number (§12.3)

In English, a verb alone does not express person, gender, or number — these require a pronoun ("she studied"). In Hebrew, inflected verbs encode person, gender, and number directly in their spelling.

| Example | Translation |
|---|---|
| כָּתַב | he wrote |
| כָּֽתְבָה | she wrote |
| כָּתַ֫בְתִּי | I wrote |
| אֲנִי כָּתַ֫בְתִּי | I wrote (with explicit pronoun) |

---

## 12.4 Summary of Person, Gender, and Number (§12.4)

| Category | Values |
|---|---|
| **Person** | First (I, we), Second (you), Third (he, she, it, they) |
| **Gender** | Masculine, Feminine, Common (either) |
| **Number** | Singular, Plural |

---

## 12.5 Introduction to Verbal Stems (§12.5)

The Hebrew verbal system has **seven major stems**: Qal, Niphal, Hiphil, Hophal, Piel, Pual, and Hithpael. The **Qal** is the basic stem; the remaining six are **derived stems** formed from it.

Verbal stems communicate two things:
- **Type of verbal action**: simple, causative, or intensive
- **Voice**: active, passive, or reflexive

**Voice** indicates whether the subject acts or is acted upon:
- **Active**: the subject performs the action ("David defeated the enemy")
- **Passive**: the subject receives the action ("David was defeated")
- **Reflexive**: the subject both performs and receives the action ("David defeated himself")

---

## 12.6 English and Hebrew Spelling of the Verbal Stems (§12.6)

The names of the derived stems are constructed on the verbal root פעל, inflected in the Perfect 3ms form.

| English | Hebrew Spelling | Diagnostics |
|---|---|---|
| Qal | קַל | unaugmented root |
| Niphal | נִפְעַל | נִ prefix; Pathach stem vowel |
| Hiphil | הִפְעִיל | הִ prefix; Hireq Yod stem vowel |
| Hophal | הָפְעַל | הָ prefix; Pathach stem vowel |
| Piel | פִּעֵל | Hireq-Tsere vowel pattern; Daghesh Forte in 2nd root consonant |
| Pual | פֻּעַל | Qibbuts-Pathach vowel pattern; Daghesh Forte in 2nd root consonant |
| Hithpael | הִתְפַּעֵל | הִתְ prefix; Tsere stem vowel; Daghesh Forte in 2nd root consonant |

The **stem vowel** is the vowel associated with the second consonant of the verbal root.

---

## 12.7 The Hebrew Verbal Stems (§12.7)

**1. Qal** — Simple action, active voice. The basic, unaugmented stem. Example: שָׁמַע — "he heard."

**2. Niphal** — Simple action, passive or reflexive voice. Prefix: נִ. Example: נִשְׁמַע — "he was heard" (passive) or "he heard himself" (reflexive).

**3. Hiphil** — Causative action, active voice. Prefix: הִ; Hireq Yod stem vowel. Example: הִמְלִיךְ — "he caused to reign / he made (someone) king."

**4. Hophal** — Causative action, passive voice (the passive of Hiphil). Prefix: הָ (Qamets Hatuf, not Qamets). Example: הָמְלַךְ — "he was made king."

**5. Piel** — Intensive action, active voice. Daghesh Forte in the second root consonant; distinctive vowel pattern. Example: שִׁבֵּר — "he smashed into pieces."

**6. Pual** — Intensive action, passive voice (the passive of Piel). Daghesh Forte in the second root consonant; Qibbuts-Pathach vowel pattern. Example: שֻׁבַּר — "he (it) was smashed into pieces."

**7. Hithpael** — Intensive action, reflexive voice. Prefix: הִתְ; Daghesh Forte in the second root consonant. Example: הִתְחַבֵּא — "he hid himself."

## 12.9 Summary of Verbal Stem Translation Values (§12.9)

| Verbal Stem | Verbal Form | Basic Translation |
|---|---|---|
| Qal | שָׁמַע | he heard |
| Niphal | נִשְׁמַע | he was heard |
| Hiphil | הִמְלִיךְ | he made (someone) king |
| Hophal | הָמְלַךְ | he was made king |

Most Hebrew verbs are not attested in all seven stems; always consult a lexicon for which stems a verb uses.

---

## 12.10 Verbal Stems and Verbal Meaning (§12.10)

A verb's meaning may differ across stems. Example: שָׁבַר = "to break" (Qal); שִׁבֵּר = "to smash into pieces" (Piel). Sometimes the relationship between meanings is not immediately apparent — e.g., בָּרַךְ means "to kneel" (Qal) but "to bless" (Piel). Always consult a lexicon.

---

## 12.11 Introduction to the Verbal Conjugations (§12.11)

Hebrew has six basic **verbal conjugations**:

1. **Perfect** (suffix conjugation) — expresses completed action or state of being. Translated by English past, present perfect, past perfect, or future perfect tenses. Signifies **aspect**: action viewed as concluded.

2. **Imperfect** (prefix conjugation) — expresses incomplete action; translated by English present or future tense, habitual/customary action, or modal values (would, could, should, may, might, can). Signifies **aspect**: action viewed as not yet concluded.

3. **Imperative** — expresses direct commands; occurs only in the second person.

4. **Infinitive Construct** — a verbal noun; often translated "to + verb." May function as subject or object of a verb.

5. **Infinitive Absolute** — no real English counterpart. Used to emphasize verbal action, substitute for an Imperative, or express simultaneous action.

6. **Participle** — a verbal adjective with both verbal and adjectival characteristics. Used attributively, predicatively, or substantively.

---

## 12.12 Strong and Weak Verbs (§12.12)

**Strong verbs** have no weak consonants (e.g., קָטַל, כָּתַב, קָבַץ).

**Weak verbs** have at least one weak root consonant — gutturals, ר, and in certain positions י and נ.

**Special weak classes:**
- **Biconsonantal (Hollow / II-ו/י)**: two root consonants, e.g., קָם (he arose)
- **Geminate**: second and third root consonants are identical, e.g., סָבַב (he surrounded)

---

## 12.13 Classification of Weak Verbal Roots (§12.13)

| Weak Verb Class | Example | Description |
|---|---|---|
| I-Guttural | עָמַד | guttural in first root position |
| II-Guttural | גָּאַל | guttural in second root position |
| III-ע/ח | בָּרַח | ח or ע in third root position |
| III-א | מָצָא | א in third root position |
| III-ה | בָּנָה | ה in third root position |
| I-י | יָשַׁב | י in first root position |
| I-נ | נָפַל | נ in first root position |
| Doubly Weak | עָלָה | multiple weak consonants (e.g., I-Guttural and III-ה) |
| Biconsonantal | קָם | only two root consonants |
| Geminate | סָבַב | identical second and third root consonants |

## 12.14 Word Order in the Hebrew Verbal Sentence (§12.14)

Normal Hebrew verbal sentence order is **verb–subject–object** (not subject–verb–object as in English).

> בָּרָא אֱלֹהִים אֵת הַשָּׁמַיִם וְאֵת הָאָרֶץ
> God created the heavens and the earth. (Gen 1:1)

The direct object may be fronted for emphasis:

> אֶת־יְהוָה אֱלֹהֶיךָ תִּירָא
> Yahweh your God you shall fear. (Deut 10:20)

---

## 12.15 Parsing Hebrew Verbs (§12.15)

**Parsing** identifies: stem, conjugation, person, gender, number, and lexical form (or verbal root).

Example: נִשְׁמַע = Niphal Perfect 3ms from שָׁמַע (lexical form) or שׁמע (verbal root).

---

## 12.16 Lexical Form for Hebrew Verbs (§12.16)

The **lexical form** of a triconsonantal verb is the **Qal Perfect 3ms** (e.g., שָׁמַע). Biconsonantal verbs use the Infinitive Construct as their lexical form (e.g., קוּם, שִׂים, בּוֹא).

---

## 12.17 Summary (§12.17)

1. Verbs describe action or state of being.
2. Most Hebrew verbs are triconsonantal; roots generate nouns and verbs with related meanings.
3. Hebrew verbs encode person, gender, and number by inflection.
4. Seven verbal stems (Qal + six derived) indicate verbal action (simple, causative, intensive) and voice (active, passive, reflexive).
5. Six conjugations: Perfect (suffix), Imperfect (prefix), Imperative, Infinitive Construct, Infinitive Absolute, Participle.
6. Strong verbs have no weak consonants; weak verbs have at least one.
7. Normal Hebrew verbal word order: verb–subject–object.
8. Parsing: stem, conjugation, person, gender, number, and lexical form.
9. Lexical form of a triconsonantal verb = Qal Perfect 3ms.

## 12.19 Something You Should Consider: Languages are Gifts of the Holy Ghost (§12.19)

> But a man cannot rightly order his own soul unless he exercises himself day and night in the Word of God. He can do that most readily if he is well versed in such languages as Hebrew and Greek, for a right understanding of the Old Testament is difficult without the one, and a right understanding of the New is equally difficult without the other.
>
> I put Hebrew last because Latin is in general use and Greek follows conveniently. Otherwise I would willingly have given Hebrew precedence, for in many places even amongst the Greeks those who are ignorant of Hebrew forms of speech have great difficulty in attempting to draw out the true sense of Scripture.
>
> If a man would penetrate to the heavenly wisdom, with which no earthly wisdom ought rightly to be considered, let alone compared, it is with such arms that he must be equipped. And even then he must still approach with a humble and thirsting spirit.
>
> —Ulrich Zwingli
