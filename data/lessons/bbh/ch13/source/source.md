
Qal Perfect Strong Verbs


    13.1      Introduction. Of the approximately 72,000 verbs in the Hebrew Bible, over two-thirds (about 50,000) occur in the Qal stem. For this reason, significant attention will be given to the various forms of the Qal stem. Only after the Qal has been mastered will the derived stems be studied. In this grammar, memorization of paradigms will be kept to a minimum. Only in your study of the Qal stem will paradigm memorization be required. Once we move into the derived stems, no further paradigm memorization will be required. Instead, you will learn a system of diagnostic indicators whereby identification of the derived stems and their conjugations is made possible without the burden of memorizing paradigms. The use of diagnostic indicators is an important and innovative feature of this grammar.
    13.2      The Qal Stem. The Qal is the simple or basic verbal stem. Qal verbs are active in voice, though a few passive forms do exist. The Qal stem also exhibits a simple or unnuanced type of action. An example of a verb in the Qal stem is קָטַל meaning “he killed.” The simple action of the Qal stem is further divided into transitive, intransitive, and stative.
         1.      Transitive. Transitive verbs can take a direct object. In the example “the prophet wrote the book,” the word “book” is the direct object of the verb “wrote” because it receives the verbal action.
         2.      Intransitive. Intransitive verbs cannot take a direct object. The English verb “perish,” as in “the king perished in the battle,” cannot take a direct object because it is an intransitive verb. Other examples of intransitive verbs include “to live,” “to die,” and “to fast.”
         3.      Stative. Stative verbs are used to describe a state of being. In the example, “the priest is old,” the verbal construction “is old” describes the state or condition of the subject (the priest). In English, a stative (or state-of-being) idea is expressed with a form of the verb “to be” (is) and an adjective (old). In Hebrew, a stative idea may be expressed through the stative verb alone, such as כָּבֵד (to be heavy) or קָטֹן (to be small). Most stative verbs are considered to be intransitive because they cannot take a direct object.

    13.3      The Perfect Conjugation. The Perfect conjugation is the first of only two main (finite) verbal conjugations in biblical Hebrew. This conjugation is also referred to as the “suffix conjugation” because of a special set of verbal suffixes (also called sufformatives) that appear with the Perfect verb in order to indicate person, gender, and number. One use of the Perfect conjugation is to express a completed action or a state of being. When used to describe a completed action (either in reality or in the mind of the speaker), the Hebrew Perfect may be translated by the English past tense (he studied), present perfect (he has studied), past perfect (he had studied), or future perfect (he will have studied). When used to describe a state of being, it will often be translated with the English present tense (he is wise). The Hebrew Perfect may also be translated by the English present tense with verbs of perception or attitude (he knows, he loves). It must be emphasized that the Hebrew Perfect does not have tense (time of action) apart from context and issues of syntax. Rather, it signifies aspect. Verbal aspect in the Perfect signifies an action with its conclusion envisioned in the mind of the speaker or writer. To state it differently, aspect in the Perfect denotes completed action, whether in the past, present, or future.
    13.4      Introduction to Qal Perfect Strong Verbs. The following Qal Perfect paradigm must be memorized with a view to both writing and oral recitation. When writing out the paradigm, exact spelling of all consonants, vowels, and accent marks is required. When practicing oral recitation, be as clear and precise as possible. Be sure to make a distinction in pronunciation, for example, between the Qamets and Pathach. This type of precision will aid in the spelling of verbal forms encountered later. In the following paradigm, both the complete verbal paradigm and the Perfect sufformatives are listed together. This should help you to identify the distinctive endings used to indicate person, gender, and number. In every Perfect conjugation of the Qal and derived stems (Niphal, Hiphil, Hophal, Piel, Pual, and Hithpael), the sufformatives are the same. For this reason, it is important to memorize these endings now.

         1.      The presentation of forms moves from third person to second to first person and from singular to plural. Notice how the paradigm begins with the third masculine singular (3ms) form, קָטַל. This is the lexical form. In a sentence, קָטַל translates “he killed.” This is also the only Perfect form that does not have a sufformative.     13.9      Verbal Roots Ending in נ. Similar to the discussion in the preceding section, when a verbal root ending in נ receives the 1cp Perfect sufformative beginning with נ (נוּ), the two identical consonants become one consonant with Daghesh Forte. Observe, therefore, that the 1cp form of שָׁכַן (to dwell) is written as שָׁכַ֫נּוּ. You would expect to see two Nuns in this form, the last letter of the verbal root and the first letter of the 1cp sufformative. The two identical consonants, however, become one consonant; ננ is written as נּ.

    The assimilation of נ in the 1cp form is also observable in those Perfect forms that have sufformatives beginning with ת. In other words, נת can also become תּ. For example, the 1cs form of the verb נָתַן (to give) is נָתַ֫תִּי. The final נ of the verbal root has assimilated into the ת of the sufformative and its presence is represented by the Daghesh Forte (see 14.17 for the Qal Perfect paradigm of נָתַן).

    13.10      Stative Verbs: Perfect. You learned above that stative verbs describe a state-of-being.     13.12      The Negative Particle לֹא. Perfect (and Imperfect) verbs are negated with the particle לֹא, usually translated “not.” It may also be spelled לוֹא. The negative particle is always placed immediately before the verb. The following examples illustrate how this particle is used.

    שְׁמַרְתֶּם אֶת־הַתּוֹרוֹת לֹא
    You did not observe the laws.

    זָכַ֫רְתָּ אֶת־הַבְּרִית לֹא
    You did not remember the covenant.

    13.13      Summary.
         1.      The Qal stem is the simple or basic verbal stem. It is used to express simple action with an active voice.
         2.      The simple action of the Qal stem may be transitive (can take a direct object), intransitive (cannot take a direct object), or stative (describing a state of being).
         3.      The Perfect conjugation is used to express a completed action and is translated with the English past tense (also with the English present perfect, past perfect, and future perfect tenses). It may also be used to describe a state of being and, as such, will be translated with the English past or present tense.
         4.      The Perfect conjugation is also called the “suffix conjugation” because different inflectional endings or sufformatives are added to the verbal root in order to indicate person, gender, and number.
         5.      You must memorize the Qal Perfect paradigm of the strong verb. You must also memorize the Perfect sufformatives.
         6.      When a verbal root ending in ת receives a sufformative beginning with ת, the two identical consonants become one consonant with a Daghesh Forte; תת is written as (כָּרַ֫תָּ) תּ.
         7.      When a verbal root ending in נ receives a Perfect sufformative beginning with נ, the two identical consonants become one consonant with Daghesh Forte; ננ is written as (שָׁכַ֫נּוּ) נּ. This final נ of the verbal root may also assimilate into the initial ת of those sufformatives that begin with ת, that is, נת is written as (נָתַ֫תִּי) תּ.
         8.      Stative verbs are classified by their stem vowel. The stem vowel is the vowel that is associated with the second root consonant. The Pathach-Stative (גָּדַל) is inflected just like the regular strong verb. The Tsere-Stative (כָּבֵד) has a Tsere stem vowel only in the Qal Perfect 3ms. The Holem-Stative (קָטֹן) has an o-class stem vowel (Holem or Qamets Hatuf) throughout the paradigm, except in the 3fs and 3cp.
         9.      Perfect (and Imperfect) verbs are negated with the particle לֹא. This particle is always placed immediately before the verb.
         10.      Finally, it is important to understand that the use of the Perfect (suffix) conjugation described in this chapter (13.3) represents only one of several possible uses. Additional uses of the Perfect conjugation are described in chapters 17 and 23.

    13.15      Additional Information: The Particle הִנֵּה. The word הִנֵּה (also spelled as הֵן and הֶן־) is commonly translated “behold” in many, often older, translations. In vocabulary, this is the translation value that is to be memorized and it is the basic meaning given in most standard lexicons. Translated in this way, הִנֵּה could be called a demonstrative interjection, but this is only one of its many uses and nuances. This particle has no exact equivalent in English.

    While the particle הִנֵּה is used in a number of different ways, three common uses are detailed below.

         1.      The particle הִנֵּה may be used to stress the importance of something (demonstrative interjection).

    וַיַּרְא אֱלֹהִים אֶת־כָּל־אֲשֶׁר עָשָׂה וְהִנֵּה־טוֹב מְאֹד
    And God saw all that he had made, and behold, (it was) very good (Gen 1:31).

    עֵין יְהוָה אֶל־יְרֵאָיו הִנֵּה
    Behold, the eye of Yahweh is upon the ones who fear him (Ps 33:18).

         2.      The particle הִנֵּה may be used to indicate the presence of someone or something with an emphasis on immediacy and urgency (predicator of existence).

    אָנֹכִי עִמָּךְ וְהִנֵּה
    And behold, I am with you (Gen 28:15).

    הִנֵּנִי וַיֹּאמֶר
    And he said, “I am here” (Gen 22:1).
    The form הִנֵּנִי (literally, “behold me”) is the particle הִנֵּה with 1cs pronominal suffix.

         3.      The particle הִנֵּה may be used to introduce a fact or situation on which a subsequent statement is based.

    שָׁמַעְתִּי בְקֹלְכֶם הִנֵּה וַיֹּאמֶר שְׁמוּאֵל אֶל־כָּל־יִשְׂרָאֵל

    13.16      Something You Should Know: Biblical Hebrew to Modern Hebrew. The Hebrew that you have been mastering in this grammar is Biblical Hebrew. It is the language of the Hebrew Bible or Old Testament, and our reasons for learning it are immeasurable. But did you know that Biblical Hebrew is just one variant of the Hebrew language that also includes Rabbinic Hebrew, Medieval Hebrew, and Modern Hebrew? And you’ll be surprised to learn that Modern Hebrew (spoken today) was not merely a natural development of the Hebrew language through the millennia but rather an intentional reboot based on Biblical Hebrew.

    Let’s take a step back, because the development of the Hebrew language is tied closely to the history of Hebrew-speaking peoples, whether they were living in Israel or not. That is to say, the movement and development of the Hebrew language followed closely the various diaspora of the people.

    Biblical Hebrew had its beginnings in an early Canaanite language (eighteenth–seventeenth centuries BC) and its script is rooted in paleo-Hebrew (ca. ninth century BC). The spoken language seems to have persisted from the fourteenth–thirteenth centuries BC until the fall of Jerusalem in 587/6 BC. The fall and subsequent exile marked a diaspora of Hebrew-speaking people and the disappearance of the language in everyday life. The language, however, remained as a literary form. At this time, in the middle of the first millennium BC, Aramaic spread throughout the Levant as a spoken language.

    It was in the second century BC that Rabbinic Hebrew developed as a spoken language. Its use, however, was limited primarily to teachings by the Pharisees and rabbis. Rabbinic Hebrew was bolstered and celebrated by religious leaders, but it was one of many languages spoken and written during this period in Israel. In the first or second century AD, the spoken language of Rabbinic Hebrew shifted almost entirely to a literary language thereby giving way to the lingua franca at that time, Aramaic. Some notable writings come from this early period, including the Mishnah and midrashim. Jesus most certainly grew up in an Aramaic-speaking environment but he likely knew at least some Hebrew, Greek, and possibly Latin. The close of Rabbinic Hebrew as a literary language was sometime in the tenth century AD, and it was during the last few hundred years of the period that we can attribute the Hebrew portion of the Talmuds as well as midrashic and haggadic literature. Aramaic, in its various dialects, persisted as a spoken language well into the seventh century AD when it was finally supplanted by Arabic.

    The shift to Medieval Hebrew attests a more ambiguous beginning and end. Sometime during the advent of Arab domination and the close of Aramaic there was a movement to revitalize Hebrew as a spoken language. Yet Medieval Hebrew never held vitality in daily life or in literature in the same way as Biblical or Rabbinic Hebrew. The close of Medieval Hebrew is uncertain, but it is clear that no major changes occurred in the Hebrew language until the second half of the eighteenth century. The long gap is due in part to the difficulties experienced by Jewish communities across the globe and the non-Hebrew-speaking environments in which many Hebrew speakers lived.

    The transition from Medieval Hebrew to Modern Hebrew was gradual, but its kickstart was with an important publication by Ben-Yehuda in 1879. For Ben-Yehuda, revival of the spoken language of Hebrew was tied to settlement in the land of Israel. The first stage of the revival (until 1918) experienced intentional effort toward solving phonological and orthographical issues. With waves of immigrants arriving in 1905 to Israel, there was a need to create new words for a new context. Yet immigrants brought with them their own dialects and usages influenced by where they were living and struggled to adapt to the Hebrew language Ben-Yehuda dreamed of. A new stage began for the language in 1922 when Hebrew was accepted as one of Israel’s official languages (British Mandate, 1918–48). At this time the language grew rapidly, and various academic institutions and esteemed faculty were employed to grow the technical vocabulary of the language. Following 1948 and the creation of the State of Israel, the Hebrew language was fully consolidated as the country’s principal language. New immigrants quickly acquired the language in special centers called ulpanim, classrooms designed to immerse speakers into the language. In 1953 the Academy of the Hebrew Language began. Its interest was (and still is) in caring for the language as well as growing it by creating new words based on verbal roots from Biblical Hebrew.

    And here is my encouragement to you as students: Keep on delving into this rich language of the Hebrew Bible. By learning it you are participating in a long history of the language, and you are learning elements of its various forms, including the language spoken today in various parts of the world, Modern Hebrew.
