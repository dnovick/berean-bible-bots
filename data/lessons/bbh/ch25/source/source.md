# BBH Chapter 25 — The Niphal Stem: Weak Verbs

*Source: Pratico & Van Pelt, Basics of Biblical Hebrew Grammar (BBH), Chapter 25*

---

## 25.1 Introduction (§25.1)

This chapter studies weak verbs in the Niphal stem. The weak verb diagnostics build upon familiarity with strong verb diagnostics, so memorize the Niphal strong verb diagnostics (§24) before working through this chapter. Many weak verbs in the Niphal preserve the diagnostics of the strong verb — for example, III-ע/ח verbs exhibit all of the Niphal strong verb diagnostics.

## 25.3 III-א Notes (§25.3)

Most diagnostics for the Niphal strong verb are retained in III-א forms. The Imperfect, Imperative, Infinitive Construct, and Infinitive Absolute diagnostics are identical to the strong verb.

1. The נִ prefix remains in both the Perfect and Participle — a sure indicator of the Niphal stem.

2. In the Perfect, the stem vowel has changed compared to the strong verb. Where the strong verb has Pathach (נִקְטַל), III-א forms have Tsere (נִמְצֵ֫אתִי). The Tsere reduces to Shewa in the 3fs (נִמְצְאָה) and 3cp (נִמְצְאוּ). The 3ms stem vowel is Qamets (נִמְצָא). Since the stem vowel is not diagnostic, knowledge of the strong verb diagnostics is sufficient for identification of most III-א Niphal forms.

## 25.5 III-ה Notes (§25.5)

The Niphal strong verb diagnostics are retained in the Imperfect, Imperative, Infinitive Construct, and Infinitive Absolute. Only the stem vowels are affected in the Perfect and Participle.

1. In all second and first person Perfect forms, the ה of the verbal root is replaced by Tsere Yod (נִבְנֵ֫יתָ). This reflects the original III-י nature of these verbs.

2. In the Perfect 3ms (נִבְנָה) and Participle ms (נִבְנֶה), the final vowel letter aids identification of the III-ה class. In the 3fs, 3cp, and remaining Participle forms, the ה of the verbal root is lost before inflectional endings.

3. Note the following diagnostic endings for III-ה verbs across all stems:

**Diagnostic Endings for III-ה Verbs in All Stems**

| Conjugation | Ending |
|---|---|
| **Perfect** | ָה |
| **Imperfect** | ֶה |
| **Imperative** | ֵה |
| **Infinitive Construct** | וֹת |
| **Participle** | ֶה |

These endings appear only in forms without sufformatives (masculine singular forms), since all other forms take inflectional endings that replace or displace the weak ending.

## 25.7 I-Guttural Notes (§25.7)

1. Where the נ of the Niphal normally assimilates into the first root consonant (Imperfect, Imperative, Infinitive Construct, and one form of the Infinitive Absolute), the guttural rejects the Daghesh Forte. This results in **compensatory lengthening**: the Hireq preformative vowel lengthens to Tsere (יֵעָזֵב). The diagnostic Qamets under the first root consonant is preserved.

2. The Perfect and Participle forms behave exactly like the I-Guttural Qal Imperfect (compare יֶחֱזַק):

| Qal Imperfect | Niphal Perfect | Niphal Participle |
|---|---|---|
| יֶחֱזַק | נֶעֱזַב | נֶעֱזָב |

In all forms, the prefix vowel is Seghol, followed by a Hateph Seghol under the guttural. The stem vowel is Pathach in the Perfect (except 3fs and 3cp) and Qamets in the Participle (except the fs).

## 25.9 I-נ Notes (§25.9)

All strong verb diagnostics are retained in the Niphal I-נ conjugations.

1. In the Perfect, one form of the Infinitive Absolute (נִצּוֹל), and the Participle, the נ that begins the form is the **נ of the Niphal stem** — not the first root consonant. The first root consonant (נ) has assimilated into the second root consonant and appears as a Daghesh Forte (נִצַּל).

2. In the Imperfect, Imperative, Infinitive Construct, and the other form of the Infinitive Absolute (הִנָּצֵל), the first root consonant נ is retained while the Niphal's נ has assimilated into it. Correctly distinguishing which נ has assimilated is essential for identifying the stem and verbal root.

## 25.11 I-י Notes (§25.11)

1. Most verbs classified as I-י were originally I-ו. In the Niphal, this original ו reappears. In the Imperfect, Imperative, and Infinitive Construct, the ו reappears as the first root consonant with a Daghesh Forte (וּ), representing the assimilated נ of the Niphal. The diagnostics of these conjugations are identical to the strong verb.

2. In the Perfect and Participle, the original ו appears as a **Holem Waw** (וֹ). The נ of the Niphal is still present; the stem vowel is the same as in the strong verb (Pathach in the Perfect, Qamets in the Participle). The only diagnostic change is the Holem Waw prefix vowel rather than the Hireq of the strong verb.

3. The primary difficulty is root identification. Remember that I-י verbs were originally I-ו and that the ו reappears in the Niphal stem as either a consonant or a vowel letter (Holem Waw).

---

## 25.12 Other Weak Forms (§25.12)

Several other weak verb classes occur in the Niphal stem. III-ע/ח weak verb diagnostics are identical to the strong verb. Biconsonantal, Geminate, and various doubly weak forms occur infrequently and do not require separate treatment here. Mastery of the diagnostics already presented should be sufficient for identifying these forms.

## 25.15 Something You Should Know: The Covenant Name of God, Part 2 (§25.15)

> Exodus 6:1–8 is the second essential text for understanding the tetragrammaton (יהוה). This text complements Exod 3:13–15. In Exod 5:1–2, Moses and Aaron bring the Lord's message to Pharaoh:
>
> וְאַחַר בָּאוּ מֹשֶׁה וְאַהֲרֹן וַיֹּאמְרוּ אֶל־פַּרְעֹה כֹּה־אָמַר יְהוָה אֱלֹהֵי יִשְׂרָאֵל שַׁלַּח אֶת־עַמִּי וְיָחֹגּוּ לִי בַּמִּדְבָּר וַיֹּאמֶר פַּרְעֹה מִי יְהוָה אֲשֶׁר אֶשְׁמַע בְּקֹלוֹ לְשַׁלַּח אֶת־יִשְׂרָאֵל לֹא יָדַעְתִּי אֶת־יְהוָה וְגַם אֶת־יִשְׂרָאֵל לֹא אֲשַׁלֵּחַ
>
> Afterwards, Moses and Aaron went and said to Pharaoh, "Thus says Yahweh, the God of Israel, 'Let my people go that they may hold a feast to me in the wilderness.' " But Pharaoh said, "Who is Yahweh that I should obey him and let Israel go? I do not know Yahweh and I will not let Israel go."
>
> Pharaoh's declaration (לֹא יָדַעְתִּי אֶת־יְהוָה) launches a graduate-level education. Initial failure only increases the burdens of God's people, prompting Moses's strident complaint (5:22–23). God's response (Exod 6:1) reiterates the promise of deliverance:
>
> וַיֹּאמֶר יְהוָה אֶל־מֹשֶׁה עַתָּה תִרְאֶה אֲשֶׁר אֶעֱשֶׂה לְפַרְעֹה כִּי בְיָד חֲזָקָה יְשַׁלְּחֵם וּבְיָד חֲזָקָה יְגָרְשֵׁם מֵאַרְצוֹ
>
> Yahweh said to Moses, "Now you will see what I will do to Pharaoh; for with a strong hand he will send them out, and with a strong hand he will drive them out from his land."
>
> The verbs יְשַׁלְּחֵם (Piel Imperfect 3ms of שׁלח with 3mp suffix) and יְגָרְשֵׁם (Piel Imperfect 3ms of גרשׁ with 3mp suffix) emphasize the decisiveness with which Pharaoh will expel the slaves. God's declaration אֲנִי יְהוָה (I am Yahweh) functions as bookends in 6:2–8. Between these bookends, a torrent of verbs describe the Lord's dynamic redemptive activity:
>
> I am יהוה.
> I appeared to Abraham, Isaac, and Jacob.
> I established my covenant with them.
> I have heard the groaning of the people of Israel.
> I have remembered my covenant.
> I will bring you out from under the burdens.
> I will deliver you from slavery.
> I will redeem you with outstretched arm and great acts of judgment.
> I will take you to be my people and I will be your God.
> I will bring you into the land.
> I am יהוה.
>
> But not even the dynamic events of Exodus will compare with what will happen in a Bethlehem manger and on a Roman cross in Jerusalem, when Isaiah's Immanuel will burst onto the stage of human history.
>

> Professor of Old Testament Emeritus
