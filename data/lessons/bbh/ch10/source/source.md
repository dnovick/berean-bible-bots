
Hebrew Construct Chain


    10.1      Introduction. There is no word for “of” in biblical Hebrew. Rather, Hebrew expresses the “of” (possessive) relationship between two nouns by what is called the construct chain. For those who have studied Greek, the construct chain is Hebrew’s rough equivalent of the genitive case. This grammatical relationship is created by placing two or more nouns side by side. Observe the following examples.

קוֹל הָאִישׁ
the voice of the man
מֶ֫לֶךְ הָאָ֫רֶץ
the king of the land

    In the above examples, there is no explicit Hebrew word for “of.” Rather, it is implied because each set of nouns appears side by side, that is, in a construct chain. In most instances, two different translations are possible. For example, קוֹל הָאִישׁ may be translated as either “the voice of the man” or “the man’s voice.” While studying the construct chain in this chapter, the “of” translation is used throughout.

    Our study of the Hebrew construct chain is divided into two main sections: function and form. First, we will describe the function or grammar of the construct chain (10.2) and then we will consider the form or spelling of construct nouns (10.3).

    10.2      Basic Grammar of the Hebrew Construct Chain.
         1.      Construct and Absolute States. Using the imagery of a chain, there are two links in the Hebrew construct chain, the construct link and the absolute link. The first of the two nouns in the construct chain is called the construct noun and is said to be in the construct state. The second of the two nouns is called the absolute noun and is said to be in the absolute state. The absolute form of a noun is also its lexical form.

הַמֶּ֫לֶךְ
עֶ֫בֶד
↑
↑
Absolute Noun
Construct Noun

         In a construct chain, there is only one absolute noun and the absolute noun is always the last noun in the chain. However, multiple construct nouns may appear in a construct chain. You should also note that when two nouns are in construct, no other words or particles may separate them (except for the definite article on the absolute noun). In other words, the chain cannot be broken.

         2.      Construct Chains Linked with Maqqef. The nouns of a construct chain are sometimes joined by Maqqef.

בֶּן־דָּוִד
the son of David
כָּל־הָעָם
all of the people
עֵץ־הַגָּן
the tree of the garden

         3.      Definiteness and Indefiniteness. Construct chains are either entirely definite (the … of the …) or entirely indefinite (a … of a …) but the construct noun will not take the definite article. The definiteness or indefiniteness of a construct chain is determined by the definiteness or indefiniteness of the absolute noun. Therefore, a construct chain is definite if the absolute noun is definite and indefinite if the absolute noun is in definite. If the absolute noun is definite, so are all the construct nouns linked to it. If the absolute noun is indefinite, so are all the construct nouns linked to it. In the following examples, the construct chains are indefinite because the absolute nouns are indefinite.

קוֹל אִישׁ
a voice of a man
מֶ֫לֶךְ אֶ֫רֶץ
a king of a land
עֶ֫בֶד מֶ֫לֶךְ
a servant of a king

         Using the same nouns, notice how the following construct chains are entirely definite because the absolute nouns are definite.

הָאִישׁ קוֹל
the voice of the man
הָאָ֫רֶץ מֶ֫לֶךְ
the king of the land
הַמֶּ֫לֶךְ עֶ֫בֶד
the servant of the king

         Even though the construct nouns lack the definite article, they are considered to be definite because the absolute nouns are definite. Remember that a noun is considered to be definite under three circumstances: (1) if it has the definite article, as in הַמַּלְכָּה (the queen); (2) if it has a pronominal suffix, as in סוּסוֹ (his horse); or (3) if it is a proper noun, as in שְׁמוּאֵל (Samuel). Note how the following construct chains are definite. In the first example, the construct chain is definite because the absolute noun is a proper noun. In the last two examples below, the construct chains are definite because the absolute nouns have pronominal suffixes.

עֶ֫בֶד שְׁמוּאֵל
the servant of Samuel
בֵּית מַלְכִּי
the house of my king
סֵ֫פֶר אָבִ֫יהוּ
the book of his father

         4.      Multiple Construct Nouns. It has already been explained that multiple construct nouns may appear in a given construct chain, but there is only one absolute noun for each construct chain. In other words, a train may have many cars but only one caboose. In instances where multiple construct nouns appear, each construct noun is translated with the key word “of” and they all depend on the absolute noun for definiteness. In the following examples, the construct chains contain more than one noun in the construct state.

עֶ֫בֶד אֲחִי הַמֶּ֫לֶךְ
the servant of the brother of the king
מֶ֫לֶךְ כָּל־הָאָ֫רֶץ
the king of all of the earth
כָּל־מִשְׁפַּ֫חַת בֵּית־אֲבִי אִמּוֹ
all of the family of the house of the father of his mother (Judg 9:1)

         5.      Construct Nouns with Inseparable Prepositions. Although a construct noun cannot take the definite article, a construct noun may appear with one of the inseparable prepositions (בְּ, כְּ, לְ).

בְּבֵית דָּוִד
in the house of David
כְּכָל־הַגּוֹיִם
like all of the nations
לְעַם־הָאָרֶץ
to the people of the land

         Note the loss of the Daghesh Lene in the second ב of בְּבֵית and in the second כ in כְּכָל because these begadkephat consonants are now preceded by an open syllable.

         6.      Construct Chains with Attributive Adjectives. It has already been explained that the nouns of a construct chain cannot be separated by other words. For this reason, when an adjective modifies either the construct or absolute noun, it must follow the entire chain. Like any attributive adjective, it must agree with the noun it modifies in gender, number, and definiteness.

הַטּוֹב דְּבַר הַמַּלְכָּה
the good word of the queen
The adjective is masculine singular, modifying the construct noun.
הַטּוֹבָה דְּבַר הַמַּלְכָּה
the word of the good queen
The adjective is feminine singular, modifying the absolute noun.

         In the following construct chains, the adjective may modify either the construct or the absolute noun. In ambiguous constructions like these, context must be your guide.

הַטּוֹב דְּבַר הַמֶּ֫לֶךְ
the good word of the king or
the word of the good king
הַטּוֹבִים דִּבְרֵי הַמְּלָכִים
the good words of the kings or
the words of the good kings

         7.      Construct Chains with Demonstrative Adjectives. Just like attributive adjectives, demonstrative adjectives must follow the construct chain. The following examples illustrate this point. Note that אֵ֫שֶׁת is the construct form of אִשָּׁה (woman, wife).

הַזֶּה אֵ֫שֶׁת הָאִישׁ
the wife of this man
הַזֶּה אֵ֫שֶׁת הָאִישׁ הַטּוֹב
the wife of this good man
הַזֶּה דִּבְרֵי הַנָּבִיא
the words of this prophet
הַזֶּה דִּבְרֵי הַנָּבִיא הַטּוֹב
the words of this good prophet
הָאֵ֫לֶּה דִּבְרֵי הַנְּבִיאִים
these words of the prophets or
the words of these prophets

         8.      Adjectives in the Construct State. You have already learned that an adjective may be used substantively, like a noun. As such, a substantive adjective can appear, just like a noun, in the construct state. The following examples illustrate this point.

מַרְאֶה יְפַת
beautiful of appearance
(יְפַת is the fs construct of יָפֶה)
חֲכַם־לֵב
wise of heart
(חֲכַם is the ms construct of חָכָם)
יִשְׂרָאֵל קְדוֹשׁ
the Holy One of Israel
(קְדוֹשׁ is the ms construct of קָדוֹשׁ)

    10.3      The Spelling of Nouns in the Construct State. When a noun appears in the construct state, it surrenders its primary accent to the absolute noun and the entire construct chain is treated as a single accented unit. Because the construct noun surrenders its primary accent, the spelling of a construct noun often changes from its spelling in the absolute state (lexical form). The types of spelling changes encountered with most construct nouns can be grouped into two basic categories: (1) vowel reduction; and (2) a change in the ending of a construct noun. Construct nouns in this second category may also experience vowel reduction. With the following examples, only the changes under discussion are marked in red.
         1.      Vowel Reduction with Construct Nouns. Because construct nouns surrender their primary accent to absolute nouns, changeable long vowels in a construct noun will reduce.
           a.      Vowel Reduction in Closed Syllables. In the construct state, changeable long vowels in a (final) closed syllable reduce to a short vowel because the syllable becomes unaccented.

מִשְׁפָּט
judgment
➣
מִשְׁפַּט
judgment of
מִדְבָּר
wilderness
➣
מִדְבַּר
wilderness of
בֵּן
son
➣
בֶּן
son of
כֹּל
all
➣
כָּל
all of (Qamets Hatuf)

           With a few words, Tsere reduces to Pathach in a final closed syllable. For example, מִזְבֵּחַ (altar) becomes מִזְבַּח (altar of) in the construct state.

           b.      Vowel Reduction in Open Syllables. In the construct state, changeable long vowels in an open syllable reduce to Vocal Shewa (or Hateph Pathach with gutturals) because the syllable moves into the propretonic position.

נָבִיא
prophet
➣
נְבִיא
prophet of
הָמוֹן
multitude
➣
הֲמוֹן
multitude of
דָּבָר
word
➣
דְּבַר
word of

           Carefully study the last example above (דָּבָר). In this example, both long vowels have reduced in the construct state. These changes follow the standard vowel preferences listed in 3.13. In the open syllable, Qamets has reduced to Vocal Shewa (דְּבַר). In the closed syllable, Qamets has reduced to Pathach (דְּבַר).

           c.      Vowel Reduction and the First Rule of Shewa. In plural nouns like בְּרָכוֹת, the Qamets under the second root letter reduces to Vocal Shewa in the construct state according to 1b above (בְּרְכוֹת). Then, according to the first Rule of Shewa (4.11.1), two initial Vocal Shewas become Hireq followed by Silent Shewa (בִּרְכוֹת), creating an initial closed syllable.

בְּרָכוֹת
blessings
➣
בִּרְכוֹת
blessings of
דְּבָרִים
words
➣
דִּבְרֵי
words of
צְבָאוֹת
armies
➣
צִבְאוֹת
armies of

         2.      Changes with the Endings of Construct Nouns. Several types of construct nouns experience a change with their endings in the construct state. For example, a feminine singular noun like תּוֹרָה will change the ָה ending to ַת in the construct (תּוֹרָה ➣ תּוֹרַת). Construct nouns of this type may also experience vowel reduction as described in 4.8.2. For example, בָּנִים becomes בְּנֵי. These distinctive endings will identify construct nouns.
           a.      Masculine Plural and Dual Endings. In the construct state, the masculine plural and dual endings change to ֵי (Tsere Yod).

אֱלֹהִים
God
➣
אֱלֹהֵי
God of
בָּנִים
sons
➣
בְּנֵי
sons of
עֵינַ֫יִם

➣
עֵינֵי
(two) eyes of

           b.      Feminine Singular Ending. In the construct state, the feminine singular ending ָה (Qamets He) changes to ַת (Pathach Taw).

תּוֹרָה

➣

מַלְכָּה

שָׁנָה

שְׁנַת

           c.      Feminine Plural Ending. In the construct state, the feminine plural ending וֹת does not change.

מַמְלָכוֹת

➣

מִשְׁפָּחוֹת

           d.      Masculine Nouns Ending with Seghol He. In the construct state, masculine singular nouns ending with ֶה (Seghol He) change to ֵה (Tsere He).

מַחֲנֶה

           e.      Monosyllabic Nouns. In the construct state, certain (not all!) singular monosyllabic nouns add Hireq Yod to their stem. You will recall that this also occurs when these same nouns take pronominal suffixes (9.9). Be careful not to confuse this Hireq Yod with the 1cs pronominal suffix.

אָב

➣
אֲבִי

אָח

➣

פֶּה

➣
פִּי

פִּי
my mouth

           Note that the above construct nouns אֲבִי and אֲחִי have a reduced vowel, Hateph Pathach, under the first root letter. The nouns with a pronominal suffix maintain the long vowel, Qamets, under the first root letter.

         3.      Other Spelling Features. In addition to the two basic types of spelling changes that take place in construct formation, there are a few other important spelling features that require comment.
           a.      Plural Segholate Nouns. In the construct state, plural Segholate nouns begin with a short vowel in a closed syllable. This is comparable to what happens when a singular Segholate noun takes a pronominal suffix (9.8, footnote 4).

עֲבָדִים
servants
➣
עַבְדֵי
servants of
שְׁבָטִים
tribes
➣
שִׁבְטֵי
tribes of
חֳדָשִׁים
months
➣
חָדְשֵׁי
months of

           b.      Diphthongs. In the construct state, the AY diphthong (e.g., בַּ֫יִת) changes to Tsere Yod and the AW diphthong (e.g., מָ֫וֶת) changes to Holem Waw.

בַּ֫יִת
house
➣
בֵּית
house of
מָ֫וֶת
death
➣
מוֹת
death of

           c.      No Change in Spelling. Finally, it is important to note that monosyllabic nouns with an unchangeable long vowel (Holem Waw, Shureq, or Hireq Yod) and singular Segholate nouns exhibit no spelling changes in the construct state. With these nouns, identification of the construct state must be determined from the context in which they appear.

קוֹל
voice
➣
קוֹל
voice of
שִׁיר
song
➣
שִׁיר
song of
מֶ֫לֶךְ
king
➣
מֶ֫לֶךְ
king of

    10.4      Summary.
         1.      Hebrew expresses the “of” (possessive) relationship between two nouns by placing them side by side, in a construct chain. In a construct chain, there is only one absolute noun and the absolute noun is always the last noun in the chain. Multiple construct nouns may appear in a construct chain.
         2.      Basic Grammar of the Hebrew Construct Chain.
           a.      Nouns in a construct chain may be linked by Maqqef as in בֶּן־דָּוִד (the son of David).
           b.      Construct chains are either definite (the … of the …) or indefinite (a … of a …). The definiteness or indefiniteness of a construct chain is determined by the definiteness or indefiniteness of the absolute noun. The construct noun will not take the definite article. Therefore, a construct chain is definite if the absolute noun is definite, and indefinite if the absolute noun is indefinite.
           c.      Although a construct noun cannot take the definite article, a construct noun may appear with one of the inseparable prepositions as in בְּבֵית דָּוִד (in the house of David).
           d.      When two or more nouns are in construct, no other words or particles may separate them. For this reason, an attributive or demonstrative adjective must follow the construct chain while still agreeing in gender, number, and definiteness with the noun that it modifies.
         3.      When a noun appears in the construct state, it surrenders its primary accent to the absolute noun and the entire construct chain is treated as a single accented unit. The types of spelling changes encountered with most construct nouns can be grouped into two basic categories: (1) vowel reduction; and (2) a change in the ending of a construct noun.
         4.      Vowel Reduction with Construct Nouns.
           a.      Changeable long vowels in a (final) closed syllable reduce to a short vowel (בֵּן becomes בֶּן).
           b.      Changeable long vowels in an open syllable reduce to Vocal Shewa (נָבִיא becomes נְבִיא) or Hateph Pathach with gutturals (הָמוֹן becomes הֲמוֹן).
         5.      Spelling Changes with the Endings of Construct Nouns.
           a.      The masculine plural and dual endings change to ֵי (אֱלֹהִים becomes אֱלֹהֵי).
           b.      The feminine singular ending ָה changes to ַת (תּוֹרָה becomes תּוֹרַת).
           c.      The feminine plural ending וֹת does not change.
           d.      Masculine singular nouns ending with ֶה change to ֵה (שָׂדֶה becomes שְׂדֵה).
           e.      Certain singular monosyllabic nouns add Hireq Yod to their stem (אָב becomes אֲבִי).
         6.      Other Spelling Features.
           a.      Plural Segholate nouns begin with a short vowel in a closed syllable (עֲבָדִים becomes עַבְדֵי).
           b.      The AY diphthong changes to Tsere Yod (בַּ֫יִת becomes בֵּית). The AW diphthong changes to Holem Waw (מָ֫וֶת becomes מוֹת).
           c.      Monosyllabic nouns with an unchangeable long vowel (קוֹל) and singular Segholate nouns (מֶ֫לֶךְ) exhibit no spelling changes in the construct state.

    10.6      Additional Information: The Superlative. In 6.6.2, you learned that the preposition מִן may be used with כֹּל (all, each, every) in the formation of a superlative construction (מִכֹּל). This is only one of several ways that Hebrew can express the superlative idea. Two additional superlative constructions in Hebrew are detailed below.
         1.      Perhaps the most common superlative construction is created by the use of an adjective that has been made definite by the definite article, a pronominal suffix, or the construct state. Of course, not all “definite” adjectives will translate as superlatives but context will suggest the possibility.

מִן־הַקָּטֹן וְעַד־הַגָּדוֹל
(literally) “from the small one to the great one” or (superlatively) “from the smallest one to the greatest one” (1 Sam 30:19)
הַקָּטֹן אֶת־אָבִינוּ הַיּוֹם
(literally) “the young one is with our father today” or (superlatively) “the youngest one is with our father today” (Gen 42:13)
הַיָּפָה בַּנָּשִׁים
(literally) “the beautiful one among (the) women” or (superlatively) “the most beautiful one among (the) women” (Song 1:8)
בְּנוֹ הַקָּטָן
(literally) “his young son” or (superlatively) “his youngest son” (Gen 9:24)

         2.      Another superlative construction consists of two nouns in a construct chain where the construct noun is singular and the absolute noun is plural and definite. In this construction, the two nouns must be the same.

קֹ֫דֶשׁ הַקֳּדָשִׁים
“the holy of holies,” meaning the most holy place (Exod 26:33)
אֲדֹנֵי הָאֲדֹנִים
“the Lord of lords,” meaning the greatest Lord (Deut 10:17)
שִׁיר הַשִּׁירִים
“the song of songs,” meaning the greatest song (Song 1:1)

    10.7      Something You Should Know: The Covenant Donkey. Genesis 49:10–12 contains a prophecy of Messiah that describes both his sufferings and glory (cf. Luke 24:25–27, 45–48; Acts 26:22–23; 1 Pet 1:10–11). The dual theme of Messiah’s sufferings and glory is also present, for example, in Gen 3:15. One suggestion of Messiah’s sufferings in Gen 49:11 is found in the particular terms used to describe the animal that is bound to the vine. The first half of this verse reads as follows:

אֹסְרִי לַגֶּפֶן עִירוֹ
He [Shiloh] ties to the vine his foal
וְלַשּׂרֵקָה בְּנִי אֲתֹנוֹ
And to the choice vine the son of his female donkey

    The general import of this language (and of the surrounding context) is that in the messianic era the grapevines will be so luxuriant and fruitful that one can simply hitch his donkey to them without concern for their being damaged. The two terms used to describe the donkey here, עַ֫יִר and בְּנִי אֲתֹנוֹ (the Hireq Yod on בְּנִי here is a genitive ending), are picked up in Zech 9 (see further below), the primary Old Testament background for the Gospel accounts of Jesus’ triumphal entry on a donkey into Jerusalem (Matt 21:1–9; Mark 11:1–10; Luke 19:28–38; John 12:12–16).

    There is a text from Mari, a city on the upper Euphrates, dating from about the eighteenth century BC, that describes a situation in which a suzerain has sent his lieutenant to supervise a covenant ratification ceremony between two of the suzerain’s vassals. The royal servant has done his duty and is now reporting back to his lord. The lieutenant recounts in particular that in this covenant ceremony he took a certain kind of animal and killed it to seal the covenant and, specifically, to symbolize the curse that would overtake anyone who would break the covenant. The term he uses for this animal is cognate to Hebrew עַ֫יִר בֶּן אָתוֹן. Evidently, this special breed of donkey was specifically stipulated for use in covenant ratification ceremonies (cf. NIDOTTE 1:575–77). The shedding of the blood of the עַ֫יִר בֶּן אָתוֹן would ratify the covenant. It was the covenant donkey.

    It is against this background that the significance of the donkey in Gen 49:11 can be appreciated. The covenant donkey also sheds light on Zech 9 and the Gospel accounts of the triumphal entry, which associate Messiah with this particular donkey known for its role in the shedding of blood in order to ratify a covenant. In particular, Messiah’s association with the covenant donkey hints that the way to glory will be achieved through the shedding of blood. Jesus is qualified to be grantee and guarantor of the new covenant through the shedding of the blood of the covenant.

    Zechariah 9:9–12 speaks in figurative terms of Messiah, the cross, and the preaching of the gospel, the speaking of peace to the nations. Verse 9bc reads as follows:

הִנֵּה מַלְכֵּךְ יָבוֹא לָךְ
Behold, your king will come to you.
צַדִּיק וְנוֹשָׁע הוּא
He is [declared] righteous and vindicated,
עָנִי וְרֹכֵב עַל־חֲמוֹר
afflicted and riding on a donkey,
וְעַל־עַיִר בֶּן־אֲתֹנוֹת
and on a foal, son of a female donkey.

    The coming king is righteous and victorious in the accomplishment of salvation for his people (this is Messiah’s glory), but he is also afflicted and he comes riding on the covenant donkey (this is Messiah’s suffering). Here the עַ֫יִר and בֶּן אָתוֹן of Gen 49:11 come together again. By means of this terminological convergence, Zech 9:9 ties the Gen 49:11 prophecy to the triumphal entry of Jesus. Jesus’ riding on a donkey on that occasion signifies, more than his humility, that he comes as the one who sheds the blood of the covenant. This was his primary purpose as he rode into Jerusalem on that day, and this is signaled by his arrival on the covenant donkey.

    Much more evidence could be adduced in support of this interpretation. One further support is found in the fact that the context of Zech 9:9 (particularly vv. 10–12) speaks (symbolically) of Christ’s work on the cross. By emerging justified from the sufferings of the cross, Christ the glorified Messiah wins the right to offer reconciliation to those who are in rebellion against the heavenly suzerain, to speak the peace of the gospel to the nations.
