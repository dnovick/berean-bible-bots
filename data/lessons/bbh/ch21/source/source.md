# BBH Chapter 21 — Qal Infinitive Absolute

*Source: Pratico & Van Pelt, Basics of Biblical Hebrew Grammar (BBH), Chapter 21*

---

## 21.1 Introduction (§21.1)

The Infinitive Absolute occurs less frequently than any other Hebrew conjugation, with only 817 total occurrences. Like the Infinitive Construct, the Infinitive Absolute is also a verbal noun. With regard to function, however, there is no precise English equivalent to the Hebrew Infinitive Absolute.

---

## 21.2 Qal Infinitive Absolute: Strong Verb (§21.2)

The Infinitive Absolute is not inflected for person, gender, or number. There is only one form to memorize. The following is the standard form for the strong verb:

| Qal Infinitive Absolute |
|---|
| קָטוֹל |

---

## 21.3 Notes on the Qal Infinitive Absolute: Strong Verb (§21.3)

1. The vowel pattern of the strong verb also occurs in most weak verb types (see §21.4).
2. The Holem Waw stem vowel is written defectively as Holem over 50 percent of the time. In other words, קָטוֹל is more commonly spelled as קָטֹל. This variation in spelling should not present any difficulty.
3. Be careful not to confuse the alternate form of the Infinitive Absolute (קָטֹל) with the Imperative 2ms or Infinitive Construct forms (קְטֹל). The Qamets under the first root consonant will distinguish the Infinitive Absolute from these two forms.
4. Unlike the Infinitive Construct, the Infinitive Absolute does not occur with prepositional prefixes or pronominal suffixes.

---

## 21.4 Weak Forms of the Qal Infinitive Absolute (§21.4)

Most weak verbs follow the strong verb pattern:

| Class | Lexical Form | Infinitive Absolute | Defective Spelling |
|---|---|---|---|
| I-Guttural | הָלַךְ | הָלוֹךְ | הָלֹךְ |
| I-Guttural | עָזַב | עָזוֹב | עָזֹב |
| I-א | אָכַל | אָכוֹל | אָכֹל |
| II-Guttural | גָּאַל | גָּאוֹל | גָּאֹל |
| II-Guttural | בָּחַר | בָּחוֹר | בָּחֹר |
| III-ח | שָׁכַח | שָׁכוֹחַ | שָׁכֹחַ |
| III-ע | שָׁמַע | שָׁמוֹעַ | שָׁמֹעַ |
| III-א | יָצָא | יָצוֹא | יָצֹא |
| I-נ | נָהַן | נָתוֹן | נָתֹן |
| I-י | יָדַע | יָדוֹעַ | יָדֹעַ |

**Notes:**

1. Note the Furtive Pathach in שָׁכוֹחַ, שָׁמוֹעַ, and יָדוֹעַ (III-ע/ח verbs). Do not confuse this with the stem vowel. The stem vowel in these forms is still Holem Waw.
2. With III-ה verbs, the Infinitive Absolute may appear in one of two forms:

| Lexical Form | Infinitive Absolute |
|---|---|
| עָשָׂה | עָשֹׂה or עָשׂוֹ |
| שָׁתָה | שָׁתֹה or שָׁתוֹ |
| רָאָה | רָאֹה or רָאוֹ |
| חָיָה | חָיֹה or חָיוֹ |

3. With Biconsonantal verbs, the Infinitive Absolute appears with a Holem Waw or Holem stem vowel in every class. In other words, שִׂים (with Hireq Yod) and קוּם (with Shureq) appear as שׂוֹם and קוֹם in the Infinitive Absolute:

| Lexical Form | Infinitive Absolute |
|---|---|
| קוּם | קוֹם |
| שִׂים | שׂוֹם |
| בּוֹא | בּוֹא or בֹּא |
| מוּת | מוֹת |

Note: בּוֹא may be identified as either the Infinitive Construct or the Infinitive Absolute and is also the lexical form of this Biconsonantal verb. Also, the Infinitive Absolute of מוּת (מוֹת) and the construct form of מָ֫וֶת (מוֹת) are spelled the same.

## 21.5 Parsing the Infinitive Absolute (§21.5)

The Infinitive Absolute is not inflected for person, gender, or number. When parsing, identify only stem, conjugation, and lexical form.

| Form | Parsing | Lexical Form |
|---|---|---|
| הָלוֹךְ | Qal Infinitive Absolute | הָלַךְ |
| מוֹת | Qal Infinitive Absolute | מוּת |

---

## 21.6 The Use of the Infinitive Absolute (§21.6)

The Infinitive Absolute is a verbal noun with no precise English equivalent. It does not occur with prepositional prefixes or pronominal suffixes. The four most common uses are:

### 1 — Emphatic

The Infinitive Absolute immediately precedes (or rarely follows) a Perfect or Imperfect of the same root to highlight the certainty of the verbal action:

> תִּשְׁמְעוּ שָׁמוֹעַ
> you (2mp) will indeed listen (Exod 19:5)

> אָמַרְתִּי אָמוֹר
> I have surely said (1 Sam 2:30)

> תָּמוּת מוֹת תֵּדַע כִּי יָדֹעַ
> know for certain that you (2ms) will surely die (1 Kgs 2:37)

### 2 — Imperatival

The Infinitive Absolute can stand by itself and function as an Imperative:

> אֶת־הַיּוֹם הַזֶּה זָכוֹר
> Remember this day! (Exod 13:3)

> אֶת־יוֹם הַשַּׁבָּת שָׁמוֹר
> Observe the sabbath day! (Deut 5:12)

### 3 — Simultaneous

Two Infinitive Absolutes may be used together with a Perfect or Imperfect verb to express two verbal actions occurring simultaneously:

> וְאָכֹל הָלוֹךְ וַיֵּלֵֶךְ
> (literally) and he walked, walking and eating; (idiomatically) and he walked, eating as he went (Judg 14:9)

> וּבָכֹה עָלֹה וְעָלוּ
> (literally) and they went up, going up and weeping; (idiomatically) and they went up, weeping as they went (2 Sam 15:30)

### 4 — Complementary

An Infinitive Absolute may complement the main verb and carry the temporal value of that main verb, translating just like a Perfect or Imperfect:

> אֹתוֹ בָאֲבָנִים כָּל־הָעֵדָה רָגוֹם הָאִישׁ יוּמַת מוֹת
> The man shall surely be put to death; all of the congregation shall stone him with stones (Num 15:35).

> אֹתוֹ עַל כָּל־אֶרֶץ מצִרָיִם וְנָתוֹן … אֹתוֹ בְּמִרְכֶּבֶת הַמִּשְׁנֶה וַיַּרְכֵּב
> And he made him ride in the second chariot … and he set him over all the land of Egypt (Gen 41:43).

---

## 21.7 Summary (§21.7)

1. The Infinitive Absolute is not inflected for person, gender, or number. There is only one form to memorize: קָטוֹל.
2. The stem vowel may be written as Holem Waw (קָטוֹל) or defectively as Holem (קָטֹל).
3. Most weak verb forms follow the strong verb pattern. III-ע/ח verbs have Furtive Pathach (שָׁמוֹעַ); III-ה verbs occur in one of two forms (בָּכֹה or בָּכוֹ); Biconsonantal verbs typically have the Holem Waw or Holem vowel (קוֹם or שׂוֹם).
4. The Infinitive Absolute is a verbal noun with four most common uses: (1) emphatic; (2) imperatival; (3) simultaneous; and (4) complementary.

## 21.9 Additional Information: The Particles יֵשׁ and אֵין (§21.9)

### 1 — יֵשׁ (there is / there are)

The particle יֵשׁ (also spelled יֶשׁ־) expresses the existence of someone or something. It does not change form to indicate gender or number.

> יֶשׁ־שֶׁבֶר בְּמִצְרָיִם
> There is grain in Egypt (Gen 42:1).

> יְהוָה בַּמָּקוֹם הַזֶּה יֶשׁ אָכֵן
> Surely Yahweh is in this place (Gen 28:16).

The particle יֵשׁ may also express possession when followed by the preposition לְ (to):

> יֶשׁ־לִי תִקְוָה
> (literally) There is to me hope. (idiomatically) I have hope (Ruth 1:12).

> לָכֶמ אָח הֲיֵשׁ
> (literally) Is there to you a brother? (idiomatically) Do you have a brother? (Gen 43:7)

### 2 — אֵין (there is not / there are not)

The particle אֵין (also spelled אַ֫יִן) is the negative counterpart of יֵשׁ and expresses the non-existence or absence of someone or something. It is also not inflected for gender or number.

> אֵין־יוֹסֵף בַּבּוֹר
> Joseph was not in the cistern (Gen 37:29).

> ־דַּעַת אֱלֹהִים בָּאָרֶץ וְאֵין
> (And) there is no knowledge of God in the land (Hos 4:1).

The particle אֵין may also express lack of possession when followed by the preposition לְ:

> לוֹ בֵּן אֵין
> (literally) There was not to him a son. (idiomatically) He had no son (Num 27:4).

> אֵין־לָנוּ חֵלֶק בְּדָוִד
> (literally) There is not to us a portion in David. (idiomatically) We have no portion in David (2 Sam 20:1).

With some frequency, אֵין appears with pronominal suffixes: אֵינֶ֫נּוּ (3ms or 1cp) and אֵינָם (3mp). The particle אֵין may also negate a verbless clause or sentences with Participles used predicatively:

> אִתָּנוּ אֵינֶנּוּ וְהַנַּעַר
> And (as for) the young man, he is not with us (Gen 44:30).

> גַּם־אֶחָד אֵין עֹשֵׂה־טוֹב אֵין
> There is no one who does good, there is not even one (Ps 53:4 [English 53:3]).

> אֱלֹהִים אֵין עוֹד זוּלָתִי אֲנִי יְהוָה וְאֵין
> I am Yahweh and there is no other; apart from me there is no God (Isa 45:5).

## 21.10 Something You Should Know: In the Cool of the Day? (§21.10)

> Our knowledge of Hebrew is not complete, and may never be. But study of other, sister languages in the ancient Near East (cognate studies) can sometimes increase our understanding of biblical Hebrew. A case in point is Gen 3:8, a verse that has long been mistranslated and misunderstood. Cognate studies can sweep away the guesswork that has long characterized translations of this verse and give us an accurate translation that also makes more sense theologically.
>
> The crux interpretum has been the Hebrew phrase, לְרוּחַ הַיּוֹם. The traditional English translation has been "in the cool of the day" or something of that sort. This translation is based on a guess made by the Septuagint translators, who thought that רוּחַ meant "wind" and יוֹם meant "day." Hence, the sense would be something like "in the windy or breezy part of the day." St. Jerome followed suit in his Latin (Vulgate) translation, and so the standard interpretation was established.
>
> Now רוּחַ can certainly mean "wind," and יוֹם can certainly mean "day." But anyone who knows Hebrew and reads the phrase in question finds the whole expression rather odd. It is odd enough to raise the question, Can't there be some other way to translate this phrase? And there is.
>
> Cognate studies, specifically in Akkadian, have revealed a cognate word for the Hebrew יוֹם (day). It is the Akkadian word, umu, which also means "day." We call this umu1 because studies have revealed a homonym, or an umu2 in Akkadian, that means "storm." The question naturally arises, What if there is a cognate word in Hebrew, a יוֹם that means not "day" but "storm"? There is such a Hebrew cognate, rarely used in the Hebrew Bible, but attested nevertheless. Holladay notes it in his lexicon, although not in Gen 3:8.
>
> On this understanding, the translation of Gen 3:8 changes significantly. It now reads: "Then the man and his wife heard the thunderous voice of Yahweh God as he was going back and forth in the garden in the wind of the storm, and they hid from Yahweh God among the trees of the garden."
>
> Yahweh has come to judge his disobedient servants. So he comes in the first storm theophany, a manner of coming later associated in the Old Testament with the "day of Yahweh." Since he comes in stormy glory, other terms in the verse merit an altered translation. "Voice" (קוֹל) now becomes "thunderous voice," as at Sinai (Exod 19:16). "Walking" (מִתְהַלֵּךְ) now becomes "going back and forth" like the lightning flashes of Ps 77:17–19.
>
> We can now translate accurately and sensibly a verse that has long seemed puzzling. More than one sermon has portrayed God and Adam walking together in the garden "in the cool of the day," as though they were taking a pleasant after-dinner stroll. The linguistic clarification brings with it greater theological clarity. Yahweh God is coming in storm theophany to judge his disobedient servants as he will come again on the day of Yahweh — as Jesus puts it, on the clouds with great power and glory — to judge a disobedient world.
>
> Jeffrey J. Niehaus
> Professor of Old Testament
> Gordon-Conwell Theological Seminary
