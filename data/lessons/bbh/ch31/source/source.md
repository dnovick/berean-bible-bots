# BBH Chapter 31 — The Piel Stem: Weak Verbs

*Source: Pratico & Van Pelt, Basics of Biblical Hebrew Grammar (BBH), Chapter 31*

---

## 31.1 Introduction

Most weak verbs in the Piel stem maintain the diagnostic features of the strong verb. It is only II-Guttural weak verbs that exhibit any major changes in spelling. The weak verb classes III-ע/ח, III-א, III-ה, and Geminate are given for your study, but you will observe that all of the strong verb diagnostics are preserved in each weak verb class.

---

## 31.2 III-ע/ח Verbs: Diagnostics and Paradigms

The paradigm verb שָׁלַח is translated "to send, stretch out, send away, expel" in the Piel stem. Note that the Tsere stem vowel of the Piel strong verb is replaced by a Pathach stem vowel in most forms of this weak verb class. This change is due to the fact that gutturals prefer a-class vowels. However, in this weak verb class, all of the diagnostics of the Piel strong verb are present.

## 31.6 II-Guttural Verbs

With the four weak verbs just studied, the strong verb diagnostics are retained in most forms. In the Piel stem, only II-Guttural verbs exhibit any major changes compared to the diagnostics of the strong verb. These changes occur because the guttural consonant in second root position rejects the Daghesh Forte of the Piel stem, and this results in either virtual doubling or compensatory lengthening (see 5.4 for a review of these concepts). With virtual doubling, the guttural consonant rejects the Daghesh Forte but the preceding vowel is not lengthened. With compensatory lengthening, the guttural consonant rejects the Daghesh Forte and the preceding vowel is lengthened. In the patterns of compensatory lengthening, the vowel Hireq lengthens to Tsere. The following graphic illustrates these two patterns with the Piel Perfect 3ms.

| Strong Verb | II-Guttural (Virtual Doubling) | II-Guttural (Compensatory Lengthening) |
|---|---|---|
| קִטֵּל | בִּעֵר | מֵאֵן |

In the form בִּעֵר the ע rejects the Daghesh Forte but the preceding vowel is not lengthened according to the principle of virtual doubling. In the form מֵאֵן the א rejects the Daghesh Forte and the expected Hireq under the first root consonant is changed to Tsere by the process of compensatory lengthening. Piel verbs exhibit either virtual doubling or compensatory lengthening. Some verbs, however, may exhibit both patterns.

## 31.8 II-Guttural Verbs with Virtual Doubling: Notes

Most of the Piel strong verb diagnostics are present in this weak class, except the Daghesh Forte in the second root consonant.

1. When the Daghesh Forte is rejected by the guttural consonant in the second root position and the preceding vowel is not lengthened, it is called virtual doubling.
2. In the II-Guttural class of weak verbs in the Piel, the stem vowel may be either Tsere (בִּעֵר) or Pathach (נִחַם). Be aware, however, that the stem vowel is not considered to be a diagnostic feature of the Piel stem.

## 31.10 II-Guttural Verbs with Compensatory Lengthening: Notes

1. In the verb בָּרַךְ (to bless), the consonant ר in second root position is functioning like a guttural in that it rejects the Daghesh Forte of the Piel. The result is compensatory lengthening of the preceding vowel.
2. In the Perfect, Hireq is lengthened to Tsere under the first root consonant (בֵּרֵךְ). In all other conjugations, Pathach is lengthened to Qamets under the first root consonant (יְבָרֵךְ). Be certain that you understand where compensatory lengthening takes place in each conjugation.

1. The major diagnostic features of the Piel stem include: (1) Daghesh Forte in the second root consonant; (2) Pathach under the first consonant of the verbal root in every conjugation except the Perfect; and (3) Vocal Shewa under all Imperfect preformatives and Participle prefixes.

## 31.13 Additional Information: The Polel Stem

In chapter 12, you were introduced to the seven "major" stems of the Hebrew verbal system (Qal and the six derived stems). There are, however, a number of additional stems that are considered "minor" because of their relative infrequency. In this grammar, we will cover four of the "minor" verbal stems in certain Additional Information sections. These stems include the Polel, Polal (33.11), Hishtaphel (34.16), and Hithpolel (35.12).

Biconsonantal verbs do not occur in the Piel, Pual, and Hithpael stems. Instead, they occur in the Polel, Polal, and Hithpolel stems. Geminate roots may also appear in these stems. In general, the meaning of the Piel corresponds to the meaning of the Polel, the Pual to the Polal, and the Hithpael to the Hithpolel. With Biconsonantal verbs, these stems are characterized by a Holem Waw after the first consonant and the duplication of the second root consonant. For your reference, the Polel forms of the verb רוּם (to exalt, lift up) are presented below.

## 31.14 Something You Should Memorize: Psalm 106:48

Psalm 106 is a poetic account of the covenantal history of Israel. It begins and ends with the praise of God, הַלְלוּ־יָהּ (Hallelujah!). This phrase forms an inclusio around the entire psalm, and it highlights what Yahweh has done in his election, deliverance, and perseveration of Israel that is worthy of praise. Interestingly, Psalm 106 is particularly concerned with the sins of the people, which its twin historical psalm (Psalm 105) does not mention. In Psalm 106, the focus upon Israel's unfaithfulness is contrasted with God's covenantal faithfulness. Yahweh continuously chastises Israel for their iniquity, and he delivers them from oppression. The psalm presents Israel as a covenant-breaking people, but Yahweh as a covenant-keeping God. At the end of the psalm (106:47), God's perpetual fidelity leads the psalmist to cry out for God to bring his people back from exile. Then, similar to the endings of the previous psalm books (see Pss 41:13; 72:18–19; 89:52), the writer enters into doxology in 106:48 to conclude Book IV of the Psalter.

> בָּרוּךְ־יְהוָה אֱלֹהֵי יִשְׂרָאֵל מִן־הָעוֹלָם וְעַד הָעוֹלָם
> וְאָמַר כָּל־הָעָם אָמֵן הַלְלוּ־יָהּ
>
> Blessed is Yahweh, the God of Israel, from everlasting to everlasting!
> Let all the people say, "Amen!" Praise Yahweh!

The first line of this verse consists of a verbless clause beginning with the predicate, a Qal passive Participle ms from ברך. The subject is the divine name and title, "Blessed is Yahweh, the God of Israel." Two prepositional phrases joined by the conjunction וְ follow. The translation "from everlasting to everlasting" is a good one. However, it is important to understand that the noun עוֹלָם can refer not only to the distant future but also to the distant past. These two prepositional phrases could also be translated, "from eternity past to eternity future." In other words, there is no single point in time—past, present, or future—in which God is not "blessed."

The second line in this verse begins with the Qal Perfect 3ms from אמר with the Waw Consecutive followed by its subject, the construct chain כָּל־הָעָם. When appearing with the Waw Consecutive, the Perfect verb may be translated with any of the meanings of the Imperfect. In this case, the volitional nuance is to be preferred, "Let all of the people say." The object of the verb is what the people say, "Amen!" This well-known Hebrew word derives from the verbal root אמן (to be reliable, faithful, or trustworthy), and it is commonly used in oath formulas. In fact, this entire expression appears repeatedly in Deut 27:15–26, the listing of the covenant curses. This verse, and the entire psalm, concludes with the imperative summons to praise (28.12), "Hallelujah" or "Praise Yahweh!"

The covenant faithfulness of God leads the psalmist to call Israel to acknowledge the blessed nature of Yahweh, their God! Yahweh chose for himself a covenant people (105:6–11; 106:5–7), and he has chosen to save them from their sins. This is not because Israel was great (105:2), but because God is good (106:1). The grace of God portrayed in Psalm 106 is truly overwhelming, and this grace points us to the ultimate deliverance of God's people through the death and resurrection of his Son, Jesus Christ! Punishment for sin has been laid on Christ (2 Cor 5:21), and through Christ, Christians are brought out of exile and into God's presence forever. This is why every believer is able to sing, הַלְלוּ־יָהּ, "Praise Yahweh!"

*Michael G. McKelvey, Associate Professor of Old Testament, Reformed Theological Seminary*
