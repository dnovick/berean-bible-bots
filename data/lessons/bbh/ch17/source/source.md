
Waw Consecutive


    17.1      Introduction. For the Qal stem, you have now completed the study of the Perfect and Imperfect verbal conjugations. You know that the Perfect conjugation may be used to express a completed action and is usually translated by the English past tense (12.11.1; 13.3). You also know that the Imperfect conjugation may be used to express incomplete action and is usually translated by the English future or present tense (12.11.2; 15.2).

    In this chapter, you will study a special and complementary use of the Perfect and Imperfect conjugations. For example, when used in the context of biblical narrative (and sometimes in poetry), an Imperfect verb may be prefixed with a distinctive form of the conjunction וְ and translated with the values of the Perfect. This special form of the conjunction is called Waw Consecutive. When prefixed with Waw Consecutive, the Imperfect verb may be called a “consecutive” Imperfect. Similarly, when a Waw Consecutive is prefixed to a Perfect verb, it may be called a “consecutive” Perfect.

    The Waw Consecutive and the consecutive verbal forms are used primarily in narrative sequences to denote consecutive actions, that is, actions occurring in sequence. For example, the sentence “I sat down, and then I opened my book, and then I studied Hebrew” describes a sequence of consecutive actions occurring in the past. Similarly, the sentence “I will sit down, and I will open my book, and I will study Hebrew” describes logically, chronologically, and sequentially related actions occurring in the future. Below you will study both the form and function of the Waw Consecutive and the consecutive verbal forms.

    17.4      The Use of the Consecutive Imperfect. In the context of Hebrew narrative, the consecutive Imperfect is normally used for the past tense narrative sequence. In other words, when an author wanted to write about a series of actions in the past, he would use the Imperfect with Waw Consecutive. Past tense narrative sequences abound in the Hebrew Bible. Study the following constructions carefully, especially the biblical examples. Each of the following points focuses on how a past tense narrative sequence may begin.
         1.      Frequently, a past tense narrative sequence will begin with a Perfect verb followed by any number of consecutive Imperfect verbs. The Perfect verb that begins the sequence serves to signal the past tense narration. Each consecutive Imperfect verb may be temporally, logically, sequentially, or consequentially related to the preceding verb.

וְהָאָדָם יָדַע אֶת־חַוָּה אִשְׁתּוֹ וַתַּהַר וַתֵּלֶד אֶת־קַיִן

And Adam knew Eve his wife, and then she conceived, and then she bore Cain (Gen 4:1).

וּמֹשֶׁה עָלָה אֶל־הָאֱלֹהִים וַיִּקְרָא אֵלָיו יְהוָה מִן־הָהָר
And Moses went up to God, and then Yahweh called to him from the mountain (Exod 19:3).

         2.      It is not uncommon for a past tense narrative sequence to begin with the consecutive Imperfect (and not with a Perfect as above).

וַיִּקְרָא אֶל־מֹשֶׁה וַיְדַבֵּר יְהוָה אֵלָיו מֵאֹהֶל מוֹעֵד
And he called to Moses and Yahweh spoke to him from the tent of meeting (Lev 1:1).

וַיִּפְשַׁע מוֹאָב בְּיִשְׂרָאֵל אַחֲרֵי מוֹת אַחְאָב
Moab rebelled against Israel after the death of Ahab (2 Kgs 1:1).

         3.      A past tense narrative sequence may begin with the temporal modifier וַיְהִי followed by Imperfect verbs with Waw Consecutive (17.7). The form וַיְהִי is the Qal Imperfect 3ms of הָיָה (to be) with Waw Consecutive. The temporal modifier stands at the beginning of the sequence in which it appears.

וַיְהִי אַחֲרֵי מוֹת אַבְרָהָם וַיְבָרֶךְ אֱלֹהִים אֶת־יִצְחָק בְּנוֹ
And after the death of Abraham God blessed Isaac his son (Gen 25:11).

         4.      You now understand that the past tense narrative sequence is composed of a string of consecutive Imperfect verbs, that is, Imperfect verbs with Waw Consecutive. Occasionally, this sequence of consecutive Imperfect verbs may be interrupted by a Perfect in the place of an expected Imperfect with Waw Consecutive. This interruption in the narrative sequence can happen for a number of reasons, as for example, when a negative clause appears in the sequence. The negated verb interrupts the sequence with the result that the verb is no longer an Imperfect with Waw Consecutive but now appears as a Perfect following the negative particle. Note that the regular conjunction וְ is often prefixed to this negative particle (וְלֹא). After this interruption, the sequence may return to the string of consecutive Imperfect verbs. The following example illustrates a past tense narrative sequence which begins with a consecutive Imperfect (וַיֶּחֱזַק). Note that the sequence is interrupted by a negative clause (beginning with וְלֹא־שָׁמַע) and then returns to consecutive Imperfect verbs (וַיִּפֶן and וַיָּבֹא).

וַיֶּחֱזַק לֵב־פַּרְעֹה וְלֹא־שָׁמַע אֲלֵהֶם כַּאֲשֶׁר דִּבֶּר יְהוָה
וַיִּפֶן פַּרְעֹה וַיָּבֹא אֶל־בֵּיתוֹ
And Pharaoh’s heart became hard and he did not listen to them just as Yahweh had said. And Pharaoh turned and went to his house (Exod 7:22–23).

         2.      On most forms, the Waw Consecutive is spelled with Shewa (וְ). In the 2mp (וּקְטַלְתֶּם) and 2fp (וּקְטַלְתֶּן), the Waw Consecutive is spelled as Shureq (וּ) because it occurs before a consonant with Vocal Shewa (5.7).
         3.      In the Perfect with Waw Consecutive, the accent normally shifts to the final syllable in the 2ms and 1cs forms. For example, קָטַ֫לְתִּי (I killed) is accented on the second syllable but וְקָטַלְתִּ֫י (and I will kill) is usually accented on the final syllable because it is prefixed with the Waw Consecutive. This shift in accent does not occur, however, when the Perfect verb is prefixed with the regular conjunction וְ as in וְקָטַ֫לְתִּי (and I killed). The shift in accent can help to distinguish between the Waw Consecutive and the regular conjunction וְ in the 2ms and 1cs forms. This point is illustrated in the beginning of Gen 9:15, which reads: וְזָכַרְתִּ֫י אֶת־בְּרִיתִי. Given that the Waw Consecutive and the regular conjunction וְ are spelled the same with a Perfect verb, is the conjunction prefixed to זָכַרְתִּ֫י the Waw Consecutive or the regular conjunction? Should this text be translated as “I remembered my covenant” (with the regular conjunction וְ) or “I will remember my covenant” (with Waw Consecutive)? Because the Hebrew Bible locates the accent on the final syllable (וְזָכַרְתִּ֫י), the Waw should be identified as the Waw Consecutive and the clause should be translated, “I will remember my covenant.” Other contextual considerations, to be studied in the next section, will further help you to distinguish between the Waw Consecutive and the regular conjunction וְ on the Perfect.

    17.6      The Use of the Consecutive Perfect. Like the consecutive Imperfect, the consecutive Perfect is used in narrative sequences. The consecutive Perfect is used for the future tense narrative sequence. For example, Hebrew may use the consecutive Perfect for prophetic revelations that look to the future or for legal instruction that regulates future conduct. Study the following constructions carefully with special attention to the Imperfect verbs followed by the Perfect with Waw Consecutive.
         1.      Normally, the future tense narrative sequence begins with an Imperfect verb followed by any number of consecutive Perfect verbs. The Imperfect verb that begins this narrative sequence signals the future tense narration.

שֵׁשֶׁת יָמִים תַּעֲבֹד וְעָשִׂיתָ כָּל־מְלַאכְתֶּךָ
Six days you will labor and (you will) do all your work (Deut 5:13).

לְמַעַן תִּזְכְּרוּ וַעֲשִׂיתֶם אֶת־כָּל־מִצְוֹתָי וִהְיִיתֶם קְדֹשִׁים לֵאלֹהֵיכֶם
In order that you may remember and (you may) do all my commandments and (you may) be holy to your God (Num 15:40).

         2.      A future tense narrative sequence may begin with the temporal modifier וְהָיָה followed by Perfect verbs with Waw Consecutive (17.7). The form וְהָיָח is the Qal Perfect 3ms of הָיָה with Waw Consecutive. The temporal modifier stands at the beginning of the sequence in which it appears.

    אֶת־קֶשֶׁת יִשְׂרָאֵל וְשָׁבַרְתִּי בַּיּוֹם הַהוּא וְהָיָה
    And in that day I will break the bow of Israel (Hos 1:5).

         3.      The consecutive Perfect may follow an Imperative verb and be translated with the the full force of the initial Imperative. Even though you have not yet studied the Imperative verb, the following examples should be clear. The forms עֲמֹד (stand!) and שִׁמְעוּ (hear!) are both Imperatives.

    שִׁמְעוּ דְבַר־יְהוָה וְאָמַרְתָּ שָּׁם אֶת־הַדָּבָר הַזֶּה וְקָרָאתָ בְּשַׁעַר בֵּית יְהוָה עֲמֹד
    Stand (Imperative) in the gate of Yahweh’s house and proclaim there this word and say, “Hear the word of Yahweh” (Jer 7:2).

    אוֹתָם וַעֲשִׂיתֶם אֶת־דִּבְרֵי הַבְּרִית הַזֹּאת שִׁמְעוּ
    Hear (Imperative) the words of this covenant and do them (Jer 11:6).

         4.      Infrequently, the Perfect may appear with the regular conjunction וְ and not with the Waw Consecutive. In these instances, the verbal construction is translated with “and” plus the past (or present) tense of the verb, as in וְקָטַל (and he killed). There are two basic contexts in which the Perfect with the regular conjunction וְ may appear.
             a.      The Perfect with the regular conjunction וְ may occur in a short sequence of Perfect verbs. In this context, the first verb in the sequence will be the regular Perfect. The Perfect verbs that follow may be joined by the regular conjunction וְ and should be translated with the temporal value of the first Perfect verb.

    מִשְׁפָּט אָכַל וְשָׁתָה וְעָשָׂה אָבִיךָ הֲלוֹא
    As for your father, did he not eat and drink and do justice? (Jer 22:15)

    זָקַנְתִּי וָשַׂבְתִּי וַאֲנִי
    But as for me, I am old and I am gray (1 Sam 12:2).

             b.      The Perfect with the regular conjunction וְ may occur in a series of short (syntactically) parallel clauses. In this context, both clauses contain a Perfect verb, the second of which is prefixed with the regular conjunction וְ and not the Waw Consecutive.

    עֲוֹנֶךָ וְסָר זֶה עַל־שְׂפָתֶיךָ נָגַע הִנְּה
    Behold, he touched this upon your lips and he removed your iniquity (Isa 6:7).

    מִיַּד חָזָק מִמֶּנּוּ וּגְאָלוֹ יְהוָה אֶת־יַעֲקֹב כִּי־פָדָה
    For Yahweh ransomed Jacob and he redeemed him from a hand of one stronger than he (Jer 31:11).

    17.7      Translating Temporal Modifiers. Because of their frequency in certain contexts, the temporal modifiers וַיְהִי and וְהָיָה are best not translated, though the conjunction may still be translated as “and.” Some will suggest that וַיְהִי be translated, “and it came to pass that” and that וְהָיָה be translated, “and it will be that.” Given the frequency of these temporal modifiers in narrative, it is preferable to avoid these translations in the interest of good English style.

    17.9      Summary.
         1.      Perfect and Imperfect verbs with Waw Consecutive are used primarily in narrative sequences to denote consecutive actions, that is, actions occurring in logical or temporal sequence.
         2.      When an Imperfect verb is prefixed with Waw Consecutive, it will be translated with the values of the Perfect. In all but one form (1cs), the Waw Consecutive on the Imperfect consists of the conjunction ו with Pathach and a Daghesh Forte in the Imperfect preformative (וַיִּקְטֹל).
         3.      Some weak verbs experience minor spelling changes with the prefixing of the Waw Consecutive to the Imperfect. In I-א verbs like אָמַר and certain I-י verbs (as well as הָלַךְ), the stem vowel usually changes to Seghol. Biconsonantal and III-ה consecutive verbs are usually “shortened” in those forms without a sufformative (except the 1cs).
         4.      The consecutive Imperfect is commonly used for the past tense narrative sequence. This narrative sequence may begin with a Perfect verb followed by any number of consecutive Imperfects.
         5.      When a Perfect verb is prefixed with Waw Consecutive, it will be translated with the values of the Imperfect. The Waw Consecutive with the Perfect is spelled just like the regular conjunction וְ (וְקָטַל).
         6.      The consecutive Perfect is commonly used for the future tense narrative sequence (and also to express habitual-durative action). This narrative sequence may begin with an Imperfect verb followed by any number of consecutive Perfects.
         7.      Infrequently, the Perfect may appear with the regular conjunction וְ and not with the Waw Consecutive. In these instances, the verbal construction is translated with “and” plus the past tense. There are two basic contexts in which the Perfect with the regular conjunction וְ may appear: (1) in a short sequence of Perfect verbs, and (2) in a sequence of short (syntactically) parallel clauses with Perfect verbs.
         8.      A past tense narrative sequence may begin with the temporal modifier וַיְהִי (Qal Imperfect 3ms of הָיָה with Waw Consecutive) followed by Imperfect verbs with Waw Consecutive. A future tense narrative sequence may begin with the temporal modifier וְהָיָה (Qal Perfect 3ms of הָיָה with Waw Consecutive) followed by Perfect verbs with the Waw Consecutive.

    17.11      Additional Information: Waw Consecutive. Hebrew grammars will refer to Perfect and Imperfect verbs prefixed with the Waw Consecutive in a number of different ways. A brief summary of this varied terminology can help to explain some of the nuances of the consecutive forms.
         1.      Waw Consecutive. Perfect and Imperfect verbal forms prefixed with the conjunction Waw are used primarily in narrative sequences to denote consecutive actions, that is, actions occurring in logical or temporal sequence. The terminology “Waw Consecutive” is descriptive of the function of this special form of the conjunction and it is the designation employed in this grammar.
         2.      Waw Conversive. Because the Perfect and Imperfect conjugations appear to switch values when prefixed with this special form of the conjunction Waw, the designation “Waw Conversive” is descriptive of the fact that the prefixing of the conjugation appears to “convert” the value of the verbal form—Imperfect to Perfect or Perfect to Imperfect. Those forms that appear with the Waw Conversive are often called “converted” verbal forms. It should be noted, however, that the presence of the conjunction does not “convert” the verb but rather marks its special function. However, the “converted” and “conversive” terminology is helpfully descriptive of what appears to occur in terms of basic translation value.
         3.      Waw Relative. When prefixed to the Perfect or Imperfect conjugations, the conjunction “relates” the verb to a previous verb. In other words, the function of the “Waw Relative” is to relate verbs one to another, especially in terms of temporal succession. Therefore, the terminology “Waw Relative” is descriptive of the function of this conjunction at the level of sentence syntax.
         4.      Wayyiqtol and Weqatal. These terms are simply the English transliterations of וַיִּקְטֹל (wayyiqtol) and וְקָטַל (weqatal). This terminology is descriptive of the verbal construction without reference to either its translation value or its function.
         5.      Preterite. The term “preterite” is a grammatical term describing a verb that refers to a simple action in the past. This is the primary value of the Imperfect conjugation when prefixed with the Waw Consecutive. Because of this, the consecutive Imperfect verb is often referred to as the “preterite.” The term “preterite” does not, however, refer to the so-called consecutive Perfect or weqatal form.

    17.12      Something You Should Know: A word Is worth a Thousand Pictures. One of the great benefits of learning to read biblical Hebrew is that you will be able to do “word studies” in the original language in order to determine precisely what a given term means in a particular passage. Although many wonderful English translations are available, doing your own word studies on key terms in a passage often provides you with tremendous insight into a text’s meaning that cannot be gained from the English alone. In the context of Ps 137:1–9, let’s look at verses 5–6 as an example.

         If I forget you, O Jerusalem, may my right hand wither.
           May my tongue cling to the roof of my mouth,
             if I do not remember you,
           if I do not exalt Jerusalem above my chief joy.

    What does it mean to “forget” Jerusalem? Why do many English translations render verse 5b as “may my right hand forget its skill” and why does the author refer to malfunctioning body parts in verses 5–6? A word study on שָׁכַח (to forget) reveals that while this term can mean simply a temporary or permanent lapse of memory, it can also refer to abandonment. Biblically, to forget God is to abandon him (Isa 49:14), to pervert one’s ways (Jer 3:21), and to commit idolatry (Deut 4:23; Judg 3:7; Ps 44:17). In essence, to forget God is to break the covenant (for example, see Deut 8:2). This is precisely what is meant in verse 5. To forget Jerusalem would be to assimilate into Babylonian culture into which Judah had been exiled and to adopt her pagan religion.

    Second, why do many English translations render verse 5b as “may my right hand forget its skill” rather than “may my right hand wither,” as it has been translated above? This is where knowing Hebrew is particularly useful. The text of verse 5b reads תִּשְׁכַּח יְמִינִי with the verb שָׁכַח (to forget). Some argue, however, that a scribal mistake known as “graphic confusion” occurred, where כָּחַשׁ (to grow lean, wither) was mistaken for the previous verb in verse 5, שָׁכַח (to forget), and that the original text actually read תִּכְחַשׁ יְמִינִי (“may my right hand grow lean” or “may my right hand wither”). The Greek version of the Old Testament, the Septuagint, also attests to the confusion by rendering the active Hebrew verb with a passive Greek verb. How is one to determine the correct reading?

    One possible interpretation, and the one followed here, is based on the context of Ps 137:5–6 and the author’s reference to a malfunctioning tongue as a consequence of “forgetting” Jerusalem. Again, a brief word study on terms in the Old Testament, not only for “tongue” but also for body parts generally (eye, ear, tongue, hand, nose, etc.), demonstrates that mute tongues, dim eyes, deaf ears, and withering hands are often linked to idol worship! This is the message of Ps 115:4–8.

         Their idols are silver and gold,
         The work of man’s hands.

         They have mouths, but they cannot speak;
         They have eyes, but they cannot see;

         They have ears, but they cannot hear;
         They have noses, but they cannot smell;

         They have hands, but they cannot feel;
         They have feet, but they cannot walk;
         They cannot make a sound with their throat.

         Those who make them will become like them,
         Everyone who trusts in them. (NASB)

    The operative principle here is that you are what you worship! Thus the translation, “may my right hand wither” in Ps 137:5 better fits the context of idolatry in Ps 137. It also complements the following verse, “may my tongue cling to the roof of my mouth,” with which it stands in parallel. In other words, the psalmist is saying, “may I become like an immobile and mute idol if I worship one ever again!”

    While much can be learned by reading Ps 137 in English, without a knowledge of biblical Hebrew textual problems like the one above cannot be examined. Additionally, by studying the text in English only, the reader may fail to see the connections between malfunctioning body parts and idolatry and, therefore, miss the force of the author’s message.
