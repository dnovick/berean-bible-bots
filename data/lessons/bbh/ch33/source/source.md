# BBH Chapter 33 — The Pual Stem: Weak Verbs

*Source: Pratico & Van Pelt, Basics of Biblical Hebrew Grammar (BBH), Chapter 33*

---

## 33.1 Introduction

Before studying weak verbs in the Pual stem, be certain that you have memorized the Pual strong verb diagnostics in the previous chapter. As in the Piel stem, most Pual weak verbs maintain the strong verb diagnostics. Only II-Guttural verbs exhibit significant changes in the Pual. Both the III-א and III-ה verb classes below maintain all of the Pual strong verb diagnostics. Those diagnostics will not be repeated. Be certain, however, that you can identify each diagnostic feature.

## 33.4 II-Guttural Verbs

Only II-Guttural verbs exhibit any significant changes in the Pual stem. These changes occur because the guttural consonant in second root position rejects the Daghesh Forte of the Pual stem and this results in either virtual doubling or compensatory lengthening. The following graphic illustrates these two patterns with the Pual Perfect 3ms.

| Strong Verb | II-Guttural (Virtual Doubling) | II-Guttural (Compensatory Lengthening) |
|---|---|---|
| קֻטַּל | נֻחַם | בֹּרַךְ |

In the patterns of compensatory lengthening, the vowel Qibbuts lengthens to Holem. In the form בֹּרַךְ, the Holem is the result of compensatory lengthening.

## 33.6 II-Guttural Verbs with Virtual Doubling: Notes

All of the Pual strong verb diagnostics are present in this weak verb class, except the Daghesh Forte in the second root consonant. This verb exhibits virtual doubling.

## 33.8 II-Guttural Verbs with Compensatory Lengthening: Notes

This verb exhibits compensatory lengthening because the ר rejects the Daghesh Forte of the Pual. As noted above, in the patterns of compensatory lengthening, Qibbuts lengthens to Holem. In the Pual Perfect, Imperfect, and Participle of בָּרַךְ, the Holem with the first consonant of the verbal root is the result of compensatory lengthening. It is important to note that this is not the Holem of the Qal active Participle.

---

## 33.9 Summary

Once again, the diagnostic features of the Pual stem include: (1) Qibbuts under the first root consonant of every form; (2) Daghesh Forte in the second root consonant of every form; and (3) Vocal Shewa under all Imperfect preformatives and Participle prefixes. Remember that the Qibbuts under the first root consonant distinguishes the Pual from the Piel. Also, like the Piel, only II-Guttural weak verbs exhibit any significant changes in the Pual. The following chart highlights the Pual stem diagnostics studied in this chapter.

**The Pual Stem: Weak Verbs**

| Class | Perfect | Imperfect | Participle |
|---|---|---|---|
| Strong | קֻטַּל | יְקֻטַּל | מְקֻטָּל |
| III-א | מֻצָּא | יְמֻצָּא | מְמֻצָּא |
| III-ה | גֻּלָּה | יְגֻלֶּה | מְגֻלֶּה |
| II-Guttural (Virtual Doubling) | נֻחַם | יְנֻחַם | מְנֻחָם |
| II-Guttural (Comp. Lengthening) | בֹּרַךְ | יְבֹרַךְ | מְבֹרָךְ |

## 33.11 Additional Information: The Polal Stem

The Polal stem is the passive counterpart of the Polel stem (31.13). It is the equivalent of the Pual stem for certain Biconsonantal verbal roots. With Biconsonantal verbs, you will recall that these stems are characterized by Holem Waw after the first root consonant and the duplication of the second root consonant. For your reference, the Polal forms of the verb רוּם (to raise, lift up, exalt) are presented below. In the Perfect, note that the forms of the Polal are identical to the Polel, except in the 3ms (רוֹמַם). In the Imperfect, there are also a number of identical forms (2fs, 3mp, 2mp).

## 33.12 Something You Should Memorize: Psalm 119:18

Psalm 119 is the great Torah psalm that appears in book five of the Psalter. It is a massive acrostic poem (1.11) consisting of 176 verses. There are twenty-two sections in this psalm, each of which contains eight verses that begin with the same letter of the Hebrew alphabet in succession. In unparalleled fashion, this psalm sets before us the wondrous and life-giving power of the word of God, "This is my comfort in my affliction, that your word gives me life" (Ps 119:50). Psalm 119:18 appears in section three, and so it begins with Gimel, the third letter of the Hebrew alphabet.

> גַּל־עֵינַי וְאַבִּיטָה נִפְלָאוֹת מִתּוֹרָתֶךָ
>
> Open my eyes so that I might see wonderful things from your law.

This verse consists of two verbal clauses. The first clause is a request in the form of an Imperative, "Open my eyes!" The verb גַּל is Piel Imperative 2ms from גלה (to uncover, open). It is followed by its object, the dual form of עַ֫יִן (eye) with a 1cs (Type 2) pronominal suffix.

The second clause provides the reason or purpose behind the psalmist's request, "So that I might see." In Hebrew, a purpose clause may be created by the Imperative-Imperfect sequence (23.7.4). The verb וְאַבִּיטָה is Hiphil Imperfect 1cs from נבט with the conjunction וְ and ָה ending (23.12). The object of the verb is a Niphal Participle fp from פלא (to be wonderful). This Participle is used substantively as the object of the verb, "wonderful things," a designation for Yahweh's mighty acts of redemption (cf. Exod 3:20; 34:10; Josh 3:5; Judg 6:13). The verse concludes with a prepositional phrase consisting of the preposition מִן and its object תּוֹרָה with a 2ms pronominal suffix, "from your law."

From this verse we come to understand that the word of God is the record of the wondrous acts of God for the redemption of his people, and we need divine help to see it as such. These are the words of life! When we memorize the word of God, we hide it in our heart (Ps 119:11) and pray that the words of our mouth and the meditations of our heart might be pleasing in his sight (Ps 19:15[14]).
