
Qal Imperative


    18.1      Introduction. The Imperative is used primarily to express direct commands. It can also be used to grant permission or to communicate a request. The Hebrew Imperative occurs only in the second person, singular and plural. For example, “(You) hate evil, and (you) love good!” (Amos 5:15).
    18.2      Imperative Paradigm: Strong Verbs. Because the Imperative conjugation occurs exclusively in the second person, there are only four forms to study: masculine singular and plural, feminine singular and plural. These forms will be easy to memorize because they are derived from their corresponding Imperfect forms. You should think of the Imperative verbal form as an Imperfect without the preformative. The following Imperative paradigm must be memorized. The Imperative sufformatives are highlighted in red.

    18.5      Imperative Weak Verbs: Part 2. In the previous section, you studied triconsonantal weak verb Imperatives in which no root consonants were lost. Compared to the strong verb paradigm, only minor vowel changes were observed. Now we will study four triconsonantal weak verb classes in which a root consonant is lost. When encountering such a form, you will need to recover the missing consonant in order to identify the verbal root. Once again, the best way to begin reconstructing verbal roots is to know your options. As with the Imperfect conjugation, there are only four major classes that drop a root consonant in the Imperative conjugation: III-ה, I-נ, I-י, and Geminate. When you are attempting to reconstruct a triconsonantal verbal root from only two remaining consonants, use the order provided above. For example, if you were to encounter שַׁל־נְעָלֶיךָ in Exod 3:5, you would need to reconstruct the verbal root of the Imperative שַׁל in order to determine its meaning. Begin with the III-ה option, שָׁלָה (to be quiet), and you will find from the lexicon that this translation (Be quiet your shoes!) does not make sense. Next, try the I-נ option, נָשַׁל (to remove), and you will discover that this is the correct option (Remove your shoes!). Once you have correctly identified the full verbal root, you may want to check the other options in order to confirm your selection. In this case, neither the I-י form יָשַׁל (unattested) or the Geminate form שָׁלַל (Plunder your shoes!) provides a better option.

    18.9      Negation of the Imperative. To produce a negative command, Hebrew does not negate an Imperative form. Prohibitions (negative commands) are expressed with the negative particles לֹא or אַל with the second person Imperfect (15.8). For review, the particle לֹא before the Imperfect can express an absolute and permanent prohibition. The particle אַל before an Imperfect expresses an immediate and specific prohibition.
    18.10      The Particle נָא. Frequently (about 190 times), Imperatives are followed by the particle נָא, which may be translated as “please” or simply left untranslated. This particle may be connected to the Imperative with Maqqef. For example, both שְׁמֹר נָא and שְׁמָר־נָא occur and may be translated as either “observe!” or “please observe!” Note that when the Imperative is connected to the particle with Maqqef, there is a vowel change in the Imperative (Holem to Qamets Hatuf). Do not be concerned about this change, however, since the presence of the נָא particle is often an indication of the Imperative (though it may signal other forms as well).
    18.11      Translating the Imperative. When translating the Hebrew Imperative, it is not always necessary to use the second person pronoun (you). For example, שְׁמֹר may be translated as either “you observe!” or simply “observe!” English style normally prefers the shorter translation without the pronoun. Note, however, that the Imperative is always second person even if you choose not to use the second person pronoun in your translation.

    עִבְדוּ אֶת־יְהוָה
    “You (2mp) serve Yahweh!” or “Serve Yahweh!” (Ps 100:2)

    18.12      Important Verbal Sequences with the Imperative. There are many important sequences involving the Imperative conjugation. Three of the more important sequences are described below.
         1.      Several Imperative verbs may occur in succession. They may be linked with the conjunction וְ. In this construction, context will determine whether or not they are related consequentially or sequentially.

    אֶת־אָבִיךָ עֲלֵה וּקְבֹר
    Go up and bury your father (Gen 50:6).

    עֲשׂוּ וִחְיוּ זֹאת
    Do this and live (Gen 42:18).

         2.      An Imperative may be followed by a consecutive Perfect. In this sequence, the Perfect verb may carry the full force of the preceding Imperative in its translation. In such cases, the Perfect verb with Waw Consecutive and the preceding Imperative are related in terms of consecution of action.

    אֶל־עַבְדִּי לֵךְ וְאָמַרְתָּ
    Go and say to my servant (2 Sam 7:5).

    בָהָר לִפְנֵי יְהוָה צֵא וְעָמַדְתָּ וַיֹּאמֶר
    And he said, “Go out and stand on the mountain before Yahweh” (1 Kgs 19:11).

         3.      An Imperative may be followed by an Imperfect, a construction that will create a purpose or result clause. In this sequence, the Imperfect will be marked with the conjunction וְ (not Waw Consecutive) and may be translated as “so that.”

    וְנִחְיֶה וְשִׁבְרוּ־לָנוּ מִשָּׁם רְדוּ־שָׁמָּה
    Go down there and buy grain for us from there so that are might live (Gen 42:2).

    אֶל־הַמֶּלֶךְ אֹתְךָ וְאֶשְׁלְחָה הֵנָּה בֹּא
    Come here so that I may send you to the king (2 Sam 14:32).

    18.13      Summary.
         1.      The Imperative is used primarily to express direct commands. It can also be used to grant permission or to communicate a request. The Hebrew Imperative occurs only in the second person, singular and plural.
         2.      Memorization of the Qal Imperative paradigm is required.

         3.      In the strong verb, the Qal Imperative forms are related to their corresponding Imperfect forms. Remove the Imperfect preformative from the four second person forms and you have the basic Imperative paradigm. Some variation in spelling does occur in certain weak verb classes.
         4.      To produce a negative command, the Imperative is not negated. Prohibitions (negative commands) are expressed with the negative particles לֹא and אַל followed by the second person Imperfect.
         5.      Imperatives may be followed by the particle נָא, which can be translated as “please” or simply left untranslated. This particle may be connected to the Imperative with Maqqef (־נָא).
         6.     18.15      Something You Should Memorize: Psalm 115:1. One of the great joys of learning Hebrew is the ability to memorize portions of the Bible in one of the original languages in which the Scriptures have come to us. The treasury is vast, but even the smallest of texts are more desirable than gold (Ps 19:10). Consider, for example, יְהוָה צִדְקֵנוּ (Yahweh is our righteousness) from Jer 23:6 or יְשׁוּעָתָה לַיהוָה (Salvation belongs to Yahweh) from Jonah 2:10. With just a few words, the gospel of the Old Testament is set before our eyes for memorization with almost no effort at all.

    In the final section of six chapters throughout this grammar, we have suggested “Something You Should Memorize” as a means of encouragement. The selection of texts for memorization is intentional. In some instances, we have endeavored to connect the work of memorization with related sections entitled, “Something You Should Know.” In other instances, the choice was based upon grammatical and theological significance.

    Our first text for memorization comes from the opening lines of Ps 115. In this psalm, Yahweh is identified as the true source of blessing, the one in whom Israel must trust, and the maker of heaven and earth. The idols of the surrounding nations, on the other hand, are lifeless, man-made objects. For these reasons, the name of Yahweh is all glorious and the only satisfying object of true worship.

    לֹא לָנוּ יְהוָה לֹא לָנוּ כִּי־לְשִׁמְךָ תֵּן כָּבוֹד עַל־חַסְדְּךָ עַל־אֲמִתֶּךָ
    Not to us, Yahweh, not to us, but to your name give glory, because of your steadfast love, because of your faithfulness.

    In the first line of this verse, the negative particle לֹא followed by the preposition לְ with a 1cp pronominal suffix is repeated on either side of the divine name, “Not to us, Yahweh, not to us.” The second line begins with כִּי. When כִּי follows a negative clause, as it does here, it may be translated adversatively as “but.” The verb תֵּן is Qal Imperative 2ms from נתן. It is preceded by its indirect object, the preposition לְ prefixed to שֵׁם (name) with a 2ms pronominal suffix. The object of the verb follows the Imperative, “but to your name give glory.” The last line consists of two prepositional phrases joined by Maqqef to their objects, both of which appear with a 2ms pronominal suffix. These two prepositional phrases provide the reasons that glory should be given to Yahweh’s name, “because of your steadfast love, because of your faithfulness.”

    In Ps 115:8, we learn that we become what we worship. Those who worship the lifeless idols of this world will become like them and experience their fate, but those who worship the one true God, the creator of the heavens and earth, will “be transformed into his likeness” (2 Cor 3:18).
